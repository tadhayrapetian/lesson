Don’t Break the Streak

Apps turn our habits into games. Are we more motivated, or just more hooked?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand an article about motivation and technology
  • use verbs that change meaning with -ing or to + infinitive
  • design a system and evaluate it critically

Background: Gamification means using game elements such as points, badges and streaks in non-game contexts. Psychologists distinguish intrinsic motivation (enjoying the activity) from extrinsic motivation (rewards) and warn that rewards can sometimes reduce intrinsic motivation.

3

First read

6 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

Life, Level Up

Language apps, fitness trackers and even banks now use points, badges and streaks. Psychologists are asking what happens when the game ends.

Last March, Arpi was on day 612 of her language-app streak. One evening, exhausted after a long shift, she forgot to do her lesson. The next morning, the app showed a sad cartoon owl and a broken streak. ‘I felt real guilt,’ she laughs. ‘Over a cartoon! I remember thinking: this is ridiculous.’ She stopped using the app for a month.

Arpi’s experience is a perfect example of gamification: using game features like points, badges, levels and rewards in activities that aren’t games. Today it’s everywhere. Fitness apps celebrate every 10,000 steps, banks give you badges for saving, and offices use leaderboards to push sales teams. The idea is simple. Games are fun, so if we make boring tasks feel like games, people will go on doing them.

And it often works. Small, frequent rewards give the brain a pleasant feeling, which makes people want to come back. Celebrating each milestone keeps users moving forward, and many people say a streak helped them build a good habit. These designs are deliberately habit-forming, which is exactly why some people get hooked.

But psychologists warn that gamification can backfire. When people focus on points, they can lose their intrinsic motivation, the pleasure of the activity itself. A student who reads to earn badges may stop reading when the badges stop. Some users start cheating the system, doing the easiest lesson just to save their streak. And when a long streak breaks, many people quit completely.

So should we stop gamifying our lives? Not necessarily. Experts suggest remembering why you started. ‘Use the game, but don’t let it use you,’ says one behavioural scientist. Arpi went back to her app, but with a new rule. ‘I try to enjoy the language now, not the numbers,’ she says. ‘My streak is only 40 days. My Spanish is better than ever.’

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: A. Paragraph 3 explains why it works. Paragraph 4 explains how it backfires.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

Close read

10 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • Arpi started a new streak the next day.
  • Some workplaces use leaderboards to motivate staff.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

ICQ: “What does not given mean?” (The text doesn’t say.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

5

Language focus

12 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • I remember thinking: this is ridiculous.
  • One evening, exhausted after a long shift, she forgot to do her lesson.
  • She stopped using the app for a month.
  • ...if we make boring tasks feel like games, people will go on doing them.
  • I try to enjoy the language now.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • ‘I remember thinking...’ and ‘She forgot to do her lesson.’ Which is a memory of the past, and which is a task that wasn’t done?remember + -ing = a memory of a past action. forget / remember + to = a task (not) done.
  • ‘She stopped using the app.’ Did she use it after that? What would ‘She stopped to use the app’ mean?No. stop + -ing = finish an activity. stop + to = stop one thing in order to do another.
  • ‘I try to enjoy the language.’ What would ‘try reading aloud’ mean?try + to = make an effort (it’s difficult). try + -ing = experiment to see if it helps.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: verbs that change meaning: -ing or to + infinitive.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: remember / forget + to = a task you need to do. remember / forget + -ing = a memory of a past action. stop + -ing = finish an activity. stop + to = stop in order to do something else. try + to = make an effort. try + -ing = experiment. go on + -ing = continue the same thing. go on + to = move on to something new. regret + -ing = be sorry about the past. regret + to = formal, used when giving bad news.

CCQs: ‘He stopped to check his phone.’ Did he check his phone? (Yes.) ‘He stopped checking his phone.’ Does he check it now? (No.)

Students often say ‘I remember to lock the door’ to talk about a memory. Use remember locking for memories.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

8 min

Complete the sentences with the -ing form or to + infinitive of the verb in brackets. Think about the meaning.

  • Please remember to do (do) your lesson before midnight.
  • I’ll never forget seeing (see) my 1,000-day streak badge.
  • My battery was dying, so I stopped at a café to charge (charge) my phone.
  • If you can’t sleep, try putting (put) your phone in another room.
  • She regrets spending (spend) so much money on in-app rewards.
  • After finishing level one, he went on to complete (complete) the whole course.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Is sentence 2 a memory or a task?’ (A memory.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

16 min

Gamify it!

Design a game system for a real-life habit. Then test another pair’s design: how could it backfire?

Drinking more water

Eight glasses a day.

Learning new words

Ten English words every day.

Saving money

For a holiday next summer.

Less screen time

Two hours less every day.

Family housework

Everyone helps at home.

Steps

  1. Choose one habit.
  2. Design a game system: points, levels, rewards, streaks or a leaderboard.
  3. Present your design to another pair.
  4. The other pair finds two ways it could backfire. Improve your design.

Useful language

Users get a reward for...If they forget to..., they...To keep people motivated, ...This could backfire if...I’d try adding...What happens when the streak breaks?

Teacher notes. 16 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Choose a habit. Design a game system to help people build it. Think about points, rewards and streaks. You have five minutes.

Pairs. 5 min design, 3 min presentation, 4 min critique and redesign. In 1:1, design together and you play the critic.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “I remember to call him yesterday.” > “I remember calling him yesterday.”
  • “I stopped to smoke last year.” > “I stopped smoking last year.”
  • “This app makes me to study every day.” > “This app makes me study every day.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

backfirehookedmilestonestreakhabit-forming
  • I’ve had a 50-day streak of morning runs.
  • Finishing my first marathon was a big milestone in my life.
  • Social media apps are designed to be habit-forming.
  • Paying children for good grades can backfire. Some stop enjoying school.
  • After one episode, I was completely hooked on the series.

B. Writing: an app review (200-240 words)

Write a review of an app you use that has game features: a language app, a fitness app, a game or something else. Describe the features, explain how they affect your motivation, and say whether you’d recommend it.

Checklist

  • Use at least four verbs from the language focus: remember, stop, try, forget...
  • Use five words from the lesson.
  • Give the app a score and justify it.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

Reward reviews that discuss intrinsic and extrinsic motivation in the student’s own words.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
