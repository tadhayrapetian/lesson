The Dumbphone Comeback

Why some young people are trading their smartphones for simple phones

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand a short article about phone habits
  • talk about past habits with used to
  • discuss the good and bad sides of life with a basic phone

Lesson 01

Teacher's overview

Length: 60 minutes plus homework.

Main aim: Speaking: to develop fluency in a problem-solving discussion about phone habits.

Subsidiary aims: Reading for gist and detail. Vocabulary for technology habits.

Language aim: used to + base verb for past habits and states.

Background: Dumbphones are basic mobile phones with calls and texts but almost no apps. Interest in them has grown among young adults who want less screen time.

1

Warm-up

5 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. How many times a day do you think you check your phone? Guess, then look at your screen time if you can.
  2. What is the first thing you do with your phone in the morning?
  3. Could you live without your smartphone for one week? Why or why not?

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Look at the questions. Talk with your partner for three minutes.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

If students have phones, let them check their real screen-time numbers. The surprise creates interest.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

5 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

Why I Swapped My Smartphone for a ‘Dumb’ One

More young people are buying simple phones. Three of them explain why.

Five years ago, Leo, 23, used to check his phone more than 100 times a day. ‘I used to scroll in bed until 2 a.m.,’ he says. ‘Every notification felt important.’ Last year he bought a simple phone. It can call, text and play the radio. That’s it.

Leo is part of a growing trend. In the US and Europe, more people are buying basic phones, often called ‘dumbphones’. Interest is highest among people under 30. Some companies now make stylish basic phones with very few features.

Why are young people doing this? Many say their smartphone was a constant distraction. Mia, 19, a student in Manchester, didn’t use to study well. ‘I felt addicted. I couldn’t read a page without picking up my phone,’ she says. Now she reads a book a week.

Not everyone finds it easy. Without maps, Mia sometimes gets lost. Banking and train tickets are harder too. Some people worry they will miss out on plans because their friends organise everything in group chats. ‘At first I hated it,’ says Sam, 27. ‘Now I just ask people to call me.’

Most dumbphone users don’t throw their smartphones away. Many keep them at home for banking and photos. But they say the simple phone gives them something important: peace of mind. ‘I used to feel tired all the time,’ Leo says. ‘Now my evenings are mine again.’

Teacher notes. 5 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: C. The text describes the trend, the reasons and the problems.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

8 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T) or false (F)?

  • Leo used to check his phone a lot.
  • Leo’s new phone can play music from the internet.
  • Interest in basic phones is highest among people under 30.
  • Mia studies better now.
  • Sam liked his simple phone from the first day.
  • Most dumbphone users have thrown their smartphones away.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T) or false (F)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “Do you need to show where the answer is?” (Yes.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

10 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • Five years ago, Leo, 23, used to check his phone more than 100 times a day.
  • Mia, 19, a student in Manchester, didn’t use to study well.
  • I used to feel tired all the time.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Does Leo check his phone 100 times a day now?No. It was a habit in the past.
  • What form of the verb comes after used to?The base form: check, feel.
  • In the negative, do we write used to or use to?use to: didn’t use to study.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: used to + base verb.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: used to + base verb = a past habit or state that is not true now. Negative: didn’t use to + verb. Question: Did you use to + verb? We don’t use used to for one action at one time in the past.

CCQs: ‘I used to scroll in bed.’ Do I scroll in bed now? (No.) Did it happen once or many times? (Many times.)

Students often say ‘I use to...’ for the present. For present habits, use the present simple or usually.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

7 min

Complete the sentences with the correct form of used to and the verb in brackets.

  • When I was 15, I used to spend (spend) hours on video games.
  • My grandparents didn’t use to have (not have) a phone at home.
  • Where did you use to buy (you / buy) music before streaming?
  • I didn’t use to like (not like) reading, but now I love it.
  • People used to remember (remember) phone numbers. Now they don’t.
  • My brother used to be (be) shy, but now he talks to everyone.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘After didn’t, do we write use to or used to?’ (Use to.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

12 min

The 48-hour challenge

Imagine you must use a basic phone for 48 hours. No apps and no internet. Only calls and texts.

Getting around

Finding a new address in your city.

Money

Paying in shops and checking your bank account.

Friends

Making plans for the weekend.

Free time

Listening to music and watching videos.

Study and work

Finding information quickly.

Mornings

Waking up on time.

Steps

  1. Look at the six cards. Decide which will be easy and which will be hard.
  2. Tell your partner how people used to do these things before smartphones.
  3. Together, make a survival plan for the 48 hours.
  4. Decide: would you try this challenge in real life? Why or why not?

Useful language

That would be easy because...I think I’d find it hard to...Before smartphones, people used to...I used to..., but now I...We could... instead.I’d definitely try it. / I’d never try it.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Imagine you have only a basic phone for 48 hours. Look at the six cards. Which will be easy? Which will be hard? Think for two minutes, then talk to your partner.

2 min preparation, 6 min discussion, 3 min whole-class sharing. Each pair presents its survival plan. Push students to use used to when they talk about the past.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Extension: class vote. Who would really try the challenge?

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

3 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 3 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “I use to check my phone every morning.” > “I usually check my phone every morning.”
  • “I didn’t used to like reading.” > “I didn’t use to like reading.”
  • “I am addicted from my phone.” > “I am addicted to my phone.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

  • Some people are so addicted to social media that they can’t stop.

  • I don’t want to miss out on the party, so I’ll come after work.
  • Having some savings gives me peace of mind.

B. Writing: a short blog post (100-130 words)

Write a blog post called ‘My phone and me’. Describe how you used your phone in the past and how you use it now. Would you try a dumbphone? Why or why not?

Checklist

  • Use used to at least three times.
  • Use four words from the lesson.
  • Give one clear reason for your opinion.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

Strong answers compare past and present clearly and finish with a clear opinion.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
