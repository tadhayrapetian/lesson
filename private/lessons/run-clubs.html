Warm-up

6 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. Where do adults in your city meet new people?
  2. Is it easier to make friends at school or as an adult? Why?
  3. Have you ever joined a club or a class to meet people? How did it go?

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Let’s talk about making friends. Discuss these questions with your partner. Four minutes.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Keep it light. Do not ask individual students whether they feel lonely.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

6 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

Swipe Left, Run Right

Tired of dating apps and lonely after the pandemic, young people are finding connection in run clubs, supper clubs and pottery classes.

Every Tuesday at 7 p.m., more than a hundred people meet outside a coffee shop in East London. They run five kilometres, slowly, chatting the whole way. Then most of them stay for drinks. ‘I moved here a year ago and didn’t know anyone,’ says Tom, 28. ‘I wasn’t used to being alone so much. Now Tuesday is the best night of my week.’

Run clubs have exploded in popularity in cities from New York to Seoul, and many people admit they don’t join only to get fit. On social media, run clubs have been called ‘the new dating apps’. Young adults say they are tired of swiping and want to meet people in real life, without the pressure of a date.

Behind the trend is a serious problem. Health organisations have warned that loneliness is now a global health issue, and some experts call it an epidemic. Many young people socialise less than their parents did at the same age. People used to meet at churches, sports clubs and local bars, but many of these places are emptier now. Many people also work from home, so they have lost what sociologists call the ‘third place’: somewhere that isn’t home or work where you see the same faces every week.

Running solves a common problem. Meeting strangers can feel awkward, especially if you hate small talk. ‘When you run side by side, you don’t need eye contact,’ explains Maya, who started a club in Berlin. ‘You talk about the route, the weather, the pain in your legs. People bond quickly because they’re doing something together.’

Businesses have noticed. There are now paid supper clubs, friendship apps and hobby classes designed for like-minded strangers. Some critics say it’s sad that people have to pay to make friends. But Tom disagrees. ‘It’s not the money. It’s the routine,’ he says. ‘You sign up, you show up every week, and slowly you get used to seeing the same people. That’s how friendship works.’

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: A. The text links the trend to loneliness and explains why group activities work.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

10 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • Tom’s club runs very fast.
  • Some people join run clubs to meet a partner.
  • Young people today socialise more than their parents did at their age.
  • Maya’s club in Berlin is the biggest in Germany.
  • According to Maya, running side by side makes conversation easier.
  • Tom thinks paying for social events is the main problem.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “What does not given mean?” (The text doesn’t say.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

12 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • People used to meet at churches, sports clubs and local bars.
  • I wasn’t used to being alone so much.
  • ...and slowly you get used to seeing the same people.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Which sentence describes a past habit that isn’t true now?‘People used to meet at churches...’
  • Which phrase means ‘it was new and strange for me’?‘I wasn’t used to being alone.’
  • Which phrase describes a process of change? What form follows it?get used to. It is followed by -ing or a noun: get used to seeing.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: used to, be used to, get used to.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: used to + base verb = a past habit that isn’t true now. be used to + -ing / noun = be familiar with something, so it isn’t strange. get used to + -ing / noun = become familiar with something over time. In be / get used to, to is a preposition, so -ing follows.

CCQs: ‘I’m used to running in the rain.’ Is it strange for me? (No.) ‘I’m getting used to my new job.’ Is it completely normal for me yet? (Not yet. It’s becoming normal.)

Common mistake: ‘I’m used to get up early.’ After be / get used to, use -ing: I’m used to getting up early.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

8 min

Complete the sentences with used to, be used to or get used to and the verb in brackets. Change the form if you need to.

  • When I was a student, I used to go (go) to bed at 3 a.m.
  • I’ve lived alone for five years, so I am used to cooking (cook) for one person.
  • It took me months to get used to speaking (speak) to strangers in English.
  • My grandparents used to meet (meet) their friends at the market every Sunday.
  • She grew up in a village. She isn’t used to living (live) in a big city yet.
  • Don’t worry. You’ll soon get used to running (run) with a big group.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘After be used to, do we use the base verb or -ing?’ (-ing.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

16 min

The newcomer challenge

Three people have just moved to your city. They don’t know anyone. Help them build a social life.

Ana, 24

Moved for her first job. Shy. Loves books and board games. Free most evenings.

Karen, 37

Recently divorced, two children. Very little free time, only weekend mornings.

Felix, 30

From Germany. Works from home as a programmer. Doesn’t speak the local language well yet.

Steps

  1. Read about the three newcomers.
  2. Discuss what will be hard for each person. What will they have to get used to?
  3. Suggest real places, clubs or activities in your city for each of them.
  4. Discuss: is it harder to make friends as an adult? Why?

Useful language

He’ll have to get used to...She probably isn’t used to...In our city, there’s a great...Back home, she used to..., so...The hardest thing would be...Making friends as an adult is harder because...

Teacher notes. 16 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Three people have just moved to your city. Read about them. What will be hard for them? What real places in our city would you recommend? You have two minutes to read.

Pairs or threes. 2 min reading, 8 min discussing and suggesting places, 5 min whole class. Collect the best real places in your city on the board.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Real-world link: students choose one place from the board and try it before the next lesson.

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “I’m used to wake up early.” > “I’m used to waking up early.”
  • “I used to living alone now.” > “I’m used to living alone now.”
  • “It’s difficult to find friends in adult age.” > “It’s difficult to make friends as an adult.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Write an article called ‘How to make friends in [your city]’. Give newcomers practical advice: places to go, clubs to join and things they will have to get used to.

Checklist

  • Use used to, be used to and get used to correctly.
  • Use five words from the lesson.
  • Give your article a catchy title and short paragraphs.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

An article needs a title that grabs attention, a direct style and practical, specific advice.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
