Background: The EU adopted a right to repair directive in 2024. It requires manufacturers to offer repairs for some products beyond the legal warranty. The UN reports that the world produced about 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022, and less than a quarter was properly recycled.

Warm-up

6 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. What was the last thing that broke in your home? What did you do?
  2. Do you know anyone who can fix things? What can they fix?
  3. Do things last as long as they did in the past, do you think?

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Think about the last thing that broke at home. Tell your partner what happened. Then discuss the other questions.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

6 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

The Battle Over Your Broken Phone

A new movement says that if you buy something, you should be able to fix it. Big tech companies have not always agreed.

Last winter, the battery in Sam’s three-year-old phone started dying by lunchtime. He took it to a small independent repair shop near his flat to have the battery replaced. The owner shook his head. The battery was glued in, the spare parts were hard to find, and the phone was designed so that only the brand’s own service centres could repair it properly. In the end, Sam bought a new phone.

Stories like this are behind the ‘right to repair’ movement. Campaigners argue that many electronic products are less repairable than they used to be. Batteries are sealed in, screws are unusual, and software can block parts that weren’t made by the original manufacturer. Some critics even accuse companies of planned obsolescence: designing products that become slow or outdated, so customers have to buy new ones.

The cost to the planet is huge. The world produces tens of millions of tonnes of e-waste every year, and only a small part of it is properly recycled. Extending the lifespan of a phone or laptop by just a year or two can save a significant amount of energy and raw materials.

Laws are changing. In 2024, the European Union adopted a right to repair directive. It requires manufacturers to repair some products even after the warranty has ended, at a reasonable price, and to make spare parts available. Some US states have passed similar laws, and several big companies now sell repair kits, so customers can fix their own devices at home instead of having them repaired by the brand.

Not everyone is convinced. Manufacturers say that home repairs can be dangerous and that sealed designs make devices thinner and more waterproof. But repair shop owners are optimistic. ‘People used to get everything fixed: shoes, radios, TVs,’ says one. ‘We’re not inventing anything new. We’re just going back to common sense.’

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: A. Problem (1 and 2), impact (3), new laws (4), different views (5).

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

Close read

10 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • Sam’s phone battery was easy to replace.
  • Sam paid a lot of money for his new phone.
  • Some software can stop parts from other companies working.
  • Most e-waste is recycled correctly.
  • The EU law requires some repairs after the warranty ends.
  • Repair shop owners expect to earn more because of the new laws.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “What does not given mean?” (The text doesn’t say.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

12 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • He took it to a small independent repair shop near his flat to have the battery replaced.
  • ...instead of having them repaired by the brand.
  • People used to get everything fixed.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Did Sam want to replace the battery himself?No. He wanted someone else (the shop) to do it.
  • What is the structure?have / get + object + past participle.
  • Which is more informal, have or get?get.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: causative have / get something done.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: have / get + object + past participle = someone else does a service for us. Any tense is possible: I had my phone repaired. I’m getting my hair cut. I need to have my car serviced. get is more informal. We also use it for bad experiences: She had her bag stolen.

CCQs: ‘I had my laptop repaired.’ Did I repair it? (No.) Who did? (A technician.) Did I arrange it? (Yes.)

Russian and Armenian speakers often say ‘I repaired my phone’ when someone else did it. Highlight this difference.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

8 min

Rewrite the sentences with have or get something done. Use the word given.

  • A technician fixed my screen yesterday.hadI had my screen fixed yesterday.
  • Someone is going to check our heating next week.getWe’re going to get our heating checked next week.
  • A tailor shortened my trousers.hadI had my trousers shortened.
  • Someone stole Ana’s phone on the metro.hadAna had her phone stolen on the metro.
  • You should ask a mechanic to service your car.getYou should get your car serviced.
  • A company cleans our office windows every month.haveWe have our office windows cleaned every month.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘In the new sentence, who does the action: you or someone else?’ (Someone else.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

16 min

Fix, replace or recycle?

Five things are broken. Decide what to do with each one.

Laptop, 4 years old

Very slow. The battery lasts one hour. Repair: €120. New: €900.

Washing machine, 8 years old

Makes a loud noise. Repair: €80. New: €450.

Headphones, 1 year old

One side doesn’t work. Repair: not possible. New: €150.

Winter boots, 5 years old

A hole in the sole. Repair: €25. New: €120.

Phone, 3 years old

Cracked screen. Repair: €160. New: €700.

Steps

  1. For each item, decide: fix it yourself, have it repaired, replace it or recycle it.
  2. Tell your partner about the last time you had something repaired.
  3. Discuss: should repair be a legal right everywhere?
  4. Agree on three ideas to make repair more popular in your city.

Useful language

I’d have it fixed because...It’s not worth repairing.I’d rather get it repaired than...The last time I had... repaired, ...That depends on the price.In the long run, ...

Teacher notes. 16 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Five things are broken. Read the cards. For each one, decide: fix it yourself, have it repaired, replace it or recycle it. Think about price, age and the planet.

Pairs. 2 min reading, 6 min deciding, 3 min personal stories, 4 min ideas. Prices are in euros, but any currency works.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “I repaired my car in the garage.” > “I had my car repaired at the garage.”
  • “It doesn’t worth it.” > “It isn’t worth it.”
  • “I need to do my haircut.” > “I need to get my hair cut.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

manufacturerlifespane-wastewarrantyspare part
  • My laptop is still under warranty, so the repair is free.
  • The shop couldn’t find a spare part for my old printer.
  • The lifespan of a typical smartphone is about three to four years.
  • Only a small part of the world’s e-waste is properly recycled.
  • The manufacturer recalled thousands of cars because of a fault.

B. Writing: a complaint email (180-220 words)

You bought a laptop 18 months ago. It has stopped working, and the shop says it can’t be repaired. Write an email to the manufacturer. Explain the problem, say what you have done so far and ask for a repair or another solution.

Checklist

  • Use have / get something done at least twice.
  • Use five words from the lesson.
  • Use a formal tone and finish with a clear request.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

A good complaint is polite, factual and specific, and it ends with a clear request and a deadline.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
