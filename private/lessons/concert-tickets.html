The Price of a Night Out

Prices that change in minutes, queues of millions, resellers everywhere. Is buying a concert ticket broken?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • follow a podcast interview for main ideas and specific details
  • recognise spoken discourse markers like Well, Exactly and Partly
  • negotiate a fair solution to a real problem

Background: Dynamic pricing changes ticket prices according to demand. When Oasis tickets went on sale in 2024, some standing tickets rose to more than double the original price during the sale. The UK competition regulator investigated.

4

First listening

7 min

Money Matters, podcast
Your teacher will play or read the recording. Listen carefully. You don’t need to understand every word.

Audio script: Money Matters, podcast. Read it aloud at natural speed, or record it in advance with a colleague.

Sam: Welcome to Money Matters. If you’ve tried to buy a concert ticket recently, you’ll know it can be a nightmare. Today I’m talking to music journalist Priya Shah. Priya, what’s going on?

Priya: Well, a few things at once. Demand for big concerts is enormous, especially since the pandemic. Fans feel they missed out for two years, and they’re willing to pay a lot. At the same time, ticket sellers have introduced something called dynamic pricing.

Sam: Which means?

Priya: It means the price changes depending on demand, like with flights or hotel rooms. If thousands of people are trying to buy at the same moment, the price goes up, sometimes while you’re waiting in the online queue.

Sam: That’s what happened with Oasis, isn’t it?

Priya: Exactly. In 2024, when the band announced their reunion tour, some fans waited for hours, and when they finally reached the front of the queue, some standing tickets cost more than twice the original price. There was a huge backlash, and the UK competition regulator investigated.

Sam: Who defends this system?

Priya: The industry argues that dynamic pricing takes money away from touts, the people who buy tickets and resell them at crazy prices. Their argument is: if the market price is high, the artist should get that money, not a reseller.

Sam: Does that make sense to you?

Priya: Partly. Touts are a real problem. Bots can buy thousands of tickets in seconds. But the result is that the fans with the most money get in, and loyal fans on normal salaries are priced out. And many fans feel tricked, because they didn’t know the price would change.

Sam: So, what’s the solution?

Priya: Some artists are trying alternatives: fixed prices, ballots where fans register and tickets are given out randomly, or resale only at face value through official sites. Some governments are also looking at laws to cap resale prices.

Sam: Before we finish, we got a voice message from a listener, Jess.

Jess: Hi. I queued online for six hours to see my favourite band. When I got in, the cheapest ticket was three hundred pounds. I closed the laptop and cried. I’ll never pay that much, but I feel like real fans don’t matter anymore.

Sam: Priya, a final thought?

Priya: Fans have more power than they think. If enough people refuse to pay these prices, the industry will have to listen.

Listen. Answer the questions. Were your predictions right?

  • What is the main topic?
  • How does Jess feel?

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Listen to the podcast. Answer the two questions and check your predictions.

Read the script once at natural speed. Change your voice for each speaker.

Pairs compare, then check predictions.

  • What is Priya’s final message?

Say: Read the sentences first. Predict the kind of word: a noun, a number? Then listen again.

Read the script a second time. Pause after key answers if needed.

Item 1 of the multiple choice depends on the single word ‘Partly’. Highlight it.

7

Speaking: a fair ticket system

18 min

A superstar is playing three concerts in your city: 60,000 tickets, but two million people want one. Design a fair system.

Fixed price

Every ticket costs the same. Touts may resell them at high prices.

Dynamic pricing

The price follows demand. The artist earns more. Fans may be priced out.

Ballot

Fans register. Winners are chosen at random.

Verified fans

Only fans who registered early, or who bought music, can buy.

Face-value resale

You can resell your ticket, but only at the original price, on the official site.

Price cap

A law sets a maximum resale price.

Steps

  1. Choose a combination of options for your system.
  2. Discuss who wins and who loses with your system.
  3. Present it. Use at least three discourse markers from stage 6.
  4. Vote for the fairest system in the class.

Useful language

Well, the main problem is...Which means...?Exactly.Partly, but...So, our solution is...Before we finish, ...

Teacher notes. 18 min. Groups of three or four.

Say: Three concerts, 60,000 tickets, two million fans. Design the fairest system you can. Think about who wins and who loses.

Groups. 3 min reading, 10 min designing, 5 min presenting and voting.

One-to-one: you are the artist’s manager who wants maximum profit. The student must persuade you.

8

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘The price of tickets is very expensive.’ > ‘Tickets are very expensive.’ / ‘The price is very high.’
  • ‘I bought a ticket on the concert.’ > ‘I bought a ticket for the concert.’
  • ‘They sell tickets for more expensive.’ > ‘They sell tickets at higher prices.’
