First read

8 min

When the Taps Run Dry

1In early 2018, the four million residents of Cape Town were told to prepare for something no major modern city had experienced: ‘Day Zero’, the day the taps would be turned off. After three years of drought, the dams that supplied the city had fallen to dangerously low levels. Residents were asked to survive on just 50 litres of water a day, less than many people use in a single shower. Remarkably, it worked. Through strict restrictions, higher prices and a huge public campaign, consumption fell by more than half, and Day Zero never arrived.

2Cape Town was not an isolated case. In 2019, the reservoirs that supply Chennai, one of India’s largest cities, almost completely dried up, and water had to be brought in by train. In 2024, Mexico City’s main reservoir system fell to historic lows, prompting warnings that parts of the capital could face their own Day Zero. These crises are often described as natural disasters, but that framing is only partly accurate.

3Drought is a trigger, not the whole story. Experts point to a combination of factors: rapidly growing populations, ageing pipes that leak a substantial share of supply, poor planning and, increasingly, climate change, which makes rainfall less predictable. In Mexico City, for instance, a significant proportion of water is estimated to be lost through leaks before it reaches homes. Scarcity, in other words, is frequently a problem of management as much as of nature.

4There is also a hidden dimension. Most of the water we use is not the water we drink or wash with, but the ‘virtual water’ embedded in the food and products we buy. Producing a kilogram of beef can require around 15,000 litres; a cotton T-shirt, more than 2,500. Agriculture accounts for roughly 70 per cent of global freshwater use. When a dry country exports water-hungry crops, it is, in effect, exporting its water.

5Solutions exist, but none is painless. Desalination plants can turn seawater into drinking water, yet they are expensive and energy-intensive. Recycling wastewater for drinking, as Windhoek in Namibia has done for decades, works technically but can meet resistance from a public that finds the idea distasteful. Pricing water to reflect its true cost encourages saving, but risks hurting the poorest households.

6Perhaps the most important lesson from Cape Town is a psychological one. Water had always felt infinite, an invisible service that appeared at the turn of a tap. Day Zero made it visible. As one resident later put it, the crisis taught people that every drop has a source, a cost and a limit. The challenge for the rest of the world is to learn that lesson before the taps start to sputter.

Read quickly. What is the writer’s main argument?

4

Close read: inference

17 min

Read again. Choose the best answer.

  • What does the writer suggest with ‘Remarkably, it worked’?
  • Why is the ‘natural disaster’ framing ‘only partly accurate’?
  • What is the point of the example of a dry country exporting crops?
  • What problem does the writer mention with recycled wastewater?
  • What is the ‘psychological lesson’ of Cape Town?
  • How would you describe the writer’s tone?

Teacher notes. 17 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: These questions are about what the writer means, not only what the writer says. Underline the evidence for each answer.

C1 strategy: eliminate options. For each wrong answer, ask ‘Why is this wrong?’ Students must prove it from the text.

Item 6: discuss words that create the tone: remarkably, dangerously, painless, before the taps start to sputter.

Meaning from context

9 min

Work out the meaning or reference from context.

  • What does it refer to in ‘Remarkably, it worked’ (paragraph 1)?The plan to cut water use: restrictions, higher prices and the public campaign.
  • Find a word in paragraph 2 that means ‘causing something to happen’.prompting
  • What does ‘framing’ mean in paragraph 2?The way an issue is presented or described.
  • Find a phrase in paragraph 4 that means ‘in reality, although it may not seem so’.in effect

Teacher notes. 9 min. Pairs.

Say: Use the context to work out these meanings. Don’t use a dictionary.

Discuss how ‘sputter’ creates a final image. Ask: ‘Why end the article like this?’

7

Speaking: 100 days of water

20 min

Your city has 100 days of water left. You are the crisis committee. Choose a short-term and a long-term plan.

Strict limits

50 litres per person per day, with fines.

Price rises

Water costs double for heavy users.

Fix the leaks

Expensive and slow, but it saves a lot of water over time.

Desalination

A new plant on the coast. Ready in three years.

Recycled water

Treat wastewater to drinking standard. The public may object.

Bans

No watering gardens, washing cars or filling pools.

Steps

  1. Discuss each option. Who wins and who loses?
  2. Choose a package for the next 100 days and a package for the next ten years.
  3. Consider fairness: how will you protect the poorest households?
  4. Present your plan. Other groups ask one difficult question each.

Useful language

The trade-off here is...In effect, this would mean...It’s a problem of management as much as...The poorest households would bear the brunt of...In the short term, ...; in the long run, ...I’m not convinced the public would accept...

Teacher notes. 20 min. Groups of three or four.

Say: You are the city’s crisis committee. You have 100 days of water. Decide your short-term and long-term plans.

Groups. 4 min reading, 10 min deciding, 6 min presenting and questions.

Push for C1 evaluation: trade-offs, fairness, feasibility.

One-to-one: you play the mayor, who keeps raising objections about cost and public reaction.

8

Reflection and feedback

7 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘The water consumption decreased on half.’ > ‘Water consumption fell by half.’
  • ‘a big lack of water’ > ‘severe water scarcity / shortages’
  • ‘The pipes are leaking the water.’ > ‘The pipes leak water. / Water is leaking from the pipes.’
