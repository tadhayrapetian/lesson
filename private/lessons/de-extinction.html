Back from the Dead

Scientists say they can bring back lost species. The harder question is whether they should.

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand a complex article about science and ethics
  • use inversion after negative adverbials for emphasis
  • make and defend difficult ethical decisions as a group

3

First read

7 min

Read the text quickly. Which statement best reflects the writer’s overall approach?

The Return of the Dire Wolf?

In 2025, a biotech company presented three wolf pups as the first ‘de-extinct’ animals. Scientists are still arguing about what exactly was born.

In April 2025, the American company Colossal Biosciences released photographs of three white, fluffy pups and made an extraordinary claim. The animals, it said, were dire wolves, a species that had been extinct for more than ten thousand years and that a fantasy television series had made famous. Never before had a company announced the return of a lost species with such confidence, and the story spread around the world within hours.

The reality was more complicated. The scientists had not found a living dire wolf cell. Instead, they compared ancient DNA from fossils with the genome of the grey wolf, a close living relative, and used gene editing to change around twenty genes in grey wolf cells. The resulting animals are larger, with paler coats and broader heads. Critics were quick to point out that this makes them, at best, a proxy: a modified grey wolf designed to resemble its ancient cousin, not the animal itself.

Supporters of de-extinction argue that the label matters less than the potential. Not only could the same techniques help revive species such as the woolly mammoth or the dodo, but they could also protect animals that are still alive. Gene editing might make endangered species resistant to disease or increase the genetic diversity of tiny populations. Some ecologists add that certain lost species once played important roles in their ecosystems, and that restoring them could help repair damaged landscapes.

Opponents see things differently. Rarely, they say, does a new technology raise so many questions at once. Where would these animals live, when the habitats they depended on have disappeared? Who would be responsible if a revived species spread out of control or carried unknown diseases? And would the promise of bringing species back make governments less willing to protect those that still exist? Every dollar spent on mammoths, critics argue, is a dollar diverted from conservation programmes that are already underfunded.

Beneath the practical objections lies a philosophical one. To some, de-extinction looks like hubris, the belief that humans can undo any damage they cause. Only when we stop destroying habitats, these critics insist, will extinction truly be reversed. Little did the scientists who first read ancient DNA imagine that their work would one day spark such a debate. Whether it ends in a scientific triumph or an expensive spectacle remains to be seen.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 4 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: A. The announcement is only the starting point. Paragraphs 3 to 5 balance both sides, and the ending stays open.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

Close read

12 min

Read again. Answer the questions in your own words.

  • Why do critics say the pups are not really dire wolves?They were made by editing about twenty genes in grey wolf cells, so they are modified grey wolves that resemble dire wolves, not the original species.
  • What benefits do supporters see beyond reviving extinct animals?The techniques could protect endangered species, for example by making them resistant to disease or increasing genetic diversity. Restoring lost species might also repair ecosystems.
  • What problem with habitats do opponents raise?The habitats that extinct animals depended on no longer exist, so it is unclear where revived animals would live.
  • How might de-extinction harm conservation, according to critics?It could take money away from living species and make governments less willing to protect animals that still exist.
  • What does the word hubris tell us about the critics’ view?They see de-extinction as arrogant: humans believing they can fix any damage they cause.
  • What is the writer’s tone in the final sentence?Neutral and open, slightly sceptical. The outcome could be a triumph or just a spectacle.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Answer the questions in your own words. Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “Can you copy whole sentences from the text?” (No, use your own words.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Question 6 tests attitude. Accept ‘neutral’ or ‘cautiously sceptical’ if students justify it with the words triumph and spectacle.

Say: Good. Now look at how the writer creates drama and emphasis in some sentences.

5

Language focus

14 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • Never before had a company announced the return of a lost species with such confidence.
  • Not only could the same techniques help revive species such as the woolly mammoth or the dodo, but they could also protect animals that are still alive.
  • Rarely, they say, does a new technology raise so many questions at once.
  • Only when we stop destroying habitats, these critics insist, will extinction truly be reversed.
  • Little did the scientists who first read ancient DNA imagine that their work would one day spark such a debate.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • What happens to the word order after Never before, Rarely and Little?The auxiliary comes before the subject, as in a question: had a company announced, does a new technology raise, did the scientists imagine.
  • In the sentence with Only when, which clause is inverted?The main (second) clause: will extinction be reversed. The Only when clause has normal word order.
  • Why does the writer use inversion here instead of normal word order?For emphasis and drama. It is typical of formal, written and rhetorical style.

Teacher notes. 14 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: inversion after negative and limiting adverbials.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: After a negative or limiting adverbial at the start of a sentence, use question word order: auxiliary + subject + verb. Never (before), Rarely, Seldom, Little, Not only... but also, Hardly... when, No sooner... than, Under no circumstances, At no time. With Only when / Only if / Only after / Not until, the inversion is in the main clause. If there is no auxiliary, add do / does / did. The style is formal and emphatic.

CCQs: ‘Rarely do we see such talent.’ Do we often see such talent? (No.) Is the sentence formal or informal? (Formal.)

Common mistakes: forgetting the auxiliary (‘Rarely we see...’) and inverting the wrong clause after Only when (‘Only when did we stop...’).

Say: Now let’s practise it.

  • The project is expensive, and it is also risky.Not onlyNot only is the project expensive, but it is also risky.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Does the word order change?’ (Yes. The auxiliary goes before the subject.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

20 min

The ethics committee

A foundation has 100 million dollars for nature. You are its ethics committee. You cannot fund every project.

Project Mammoth, $40m

Create cold-resistant elephants with mammoth traits to live in the Arctic. Claimed benefit: restoring grasslands.

Project Thylacine, $30m

Bring back the Tasmanian tiger, extinct since 1936. Part of its old habitat still exists.

Project Northern White Rhino, $25m

Use IVF and stem cells to save a rhino with only two females left alive.

Project Rainforest, $50m

Protect 5,000 km² of rainforest, home to hundreds of endangered species.

Project Coral, $35m

Gene-edit corals so they survive in warmer seas.

Steps

  1. Read the five projects.
  2. Discuss the ethical and practical arguments for each one.
  3. Spend your 100 million dollars. Decide what you fund, fully or partly, and what you reject.
  4. Present your decision. Use at least two inversions for emphasis.

Useful language

Not only would this..., but it would also...Under no circumstances should we...Only if... would I support...The ethical red line for me is...That’s a compelling argument, but...Let’s weigh the costs against the benefits.

Teacher notes. 20 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: You’re the ethics committee of a foundation with 100 million dollars. Read the five projects. You can’t fund all of them. Decide, and be ready to defend your choices.

Groups of three or four. 4 min reading, 10 min discussion and budgeting, 6 min presentations with questions from other groups. In 1:1, the student chairs and you argue for the projects they reject.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Extension: should private companies be allowed to own the rights to a revived species?

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “Never before I have seen such a thing.” > “Never before have I seen such a thing.”
  • “Not only it is expensive, but also dangerous.” > “Not only is it expensive, but it is also dangerous.”
  • “The dinosaurs were extincted.” > “The dinosaurs became extinct.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

‘Money spent on de-extinction would be better spent on protecting living species.’ Discuss both sides of the argument and give your own reasoned view.

Checklist

  • Use at least three inversions for emphasis.
  • Use six words from the lesson.
  • Balance ethical and practical arguments.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

Inversion works best when it is rare. Two or three well-placed examples are better than six forced ones.
