  2. Which jobs do you think machines will do in twenty years?
  3. Should rich people receive the same help from the state as poor people?

3

First read

7 min

Read the text quickly. What is the main purpose of the article?

Free Money for Everyone?

Finland, Kenya and dozens of cities have tested giving people cash with no strings attached. The results are more interesting than either side expected.

Imagine receiving a fixed amount of money from the government every month, whether you work or not, whether you are rich or poor, with no forms to fill in and no questions asked. This is the idea behind universal basic income, or UBI. Once a fringe proposal, it has moved into the mainstream, driven largely by fears that automation and artificial intelligence could displace millions of workers in the coming decades.

Supporters argue that UBI would replace a confusing patchwork of welfare payments with something simple and fair. Traditional benefits are usually means-tested, which requires expensive bureaucracy and can trap people in poverty: if you take a low-paid job, you may lose your benefits and end up no better off. A universal payment avoids this problem. Provided that it was set at a sensible level, supporters say, it would act as a safety net while still rewarding work.

What do the experiments show? In 2017 and 2018, Finland gave 2,000 unemployed people a monthly payment of 560 euros with no conditions. Participants did not work much more than a comparison group, but they reported better wellbeing, less stress and more trust in institutions. In Kenya, the charity GiveDirectly has been running a long-term study in which whole villages receive regular cash. Findings so far suggest that people used the money to start businesses and invest, not to stop working. Had the Finnish trial lasted longer, some economists argue, the effects on employment might have been clearer.

Critics are not convinced. Their main objection is fiscal. A meaningful UBI paid to every adult would cost a huge share of a country’s budget, requiring much higher taxes or deep cuts elsewhere. Others fear that unconditional money creates a disincentive to work, especially in unpleasant but necessary jobs. And most trials have been small pilot schemes with limited budgets. If governments had tested UBI at a national scale, we would be in a far better position to judge it today.

For now, UBI remains a powerful idea rather than a policy. But the questions it raises are not going away. Should machines take over much of today’s work, societies will need to decide how wealth is shared. And supposing work no longer defined who we are, what would we do with our time? Many participants in the Finnish trial said they felt more confident about their future. That may be the most valuable result of all.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 4 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: B. The writer presents both sides and the evidence, and ends with open questions.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

5

Language focus

14 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • If governments had tested UBI at a national scale, we would be in a far better position to judge it today.
  • Had the Finnish trial lasted longer, some economists argue, the effects on employment might have been clearer.
  • Provided that it was set at a sensible level, supporters say, it would act as a safety net.
  • Should machines take over much of today’s work, societies will need to decide how wealth is shared.
  • And supposing work no longer defined who we are, what would we do with our time?

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • In the first sentence, which part is about the past and which is about the present?Past: if governments had tested. Present result: we would be in a better position today. This is a mixed conditional.
  • What word is missing from ‘Had the Finnish trial lasted longer’? What is the effect?if: If the Finnish trial had lasted longer. The inversion sounds more formal.
  • What do provided that, should and supposing mean here?provided that = only if. should + subject = if, perhaps by chance (formal). supposing = imagine that.

Teacher notes. 14 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: mixed conditionals, inversion and alternatives to if.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: Mixed conditionals: If + past perfect, would + base verb = past cause, present result. If + past simple, would have + past participle = present situation, past result. Formal inversion without if: Had I known... (third), Were I to... / Were it not for... (second), Should you need... (first). Alternatives to if: provided / providing (that), as long as, on condition that = only if. unless = except if. supposing = imagine. otherwise = if not. but for + noun = if it weren’t / hadn’t been for.

CCQs: ‘If I had studied economics, I would understand this article.’ Did I study economics? (No.) Do I understand the article now? (No.)

Learners mix up the halves of mixed conditionals (‘If I studied economics, I would have understood...’). Use a timeline. After provided that and as long as, don’t use will.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

Practice

10 min

Rewrite the sentences using the word given.

  • I didn’t save money last year, so I’m worried now.IfIf I had saved money last year, I wouldn’t be worried now.
  • If you need more information, please contact us.ShouldShould you need more information, please contact us.
  • If the government hadn’t helped, many families would have lost their homes.But forBut for the government’s help, many families would have lost their homes.
  • You can join the scheme only if you live in the city.providedYou can join the scheme provided (that) you live in the city.
  • If they had known the costs, they wouldn’t have started the project.HadHad they known the costs, they wouldn’t have started the project.
  • She isn’t good with money, so she didn’t invest the payment.IfIf she were good with money, she would have invested the payment.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘In sentence 1, is the result in the past or the present?’ (The present.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

20 min

The citizens’ assembly

Your city has agreed to run a two-year basic income pilot. You are the citizens’ assembly that designs it.

Amount

Enough to live on, or a small monthly top-up?

Who receives it

Every adult, only the unemployed, only young people, or one district?

Funding

Taxes on income, wealth, carbon or tech companies?

Measuring success

Employment, health, crime, wellbeing or school results?

The risks

What could go wrong, and how will you know?

Steps

  1. Decide on each of the five points.
  2. Predict the results. What would happen if...?
  3. Present your pilot to the class.
  4. Other groups act as sceptical journalists and ask hard questions.

Useful language

Provided that the payment is..., ...Were we to fund it through..., ...Had earlier trials been bigger, ...The risk is that...I’m not entirely convinced that...Let’s assume for a moment that...

Teacher notes. 20 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Your city will run a two-year basic income pilot, and you’re designing it. Decide on the five points on the cards. Then you’ll defend your design against tough questions.

Groups of three or four. 3 min reading, 10 min designing, 7 min presentations with questions. In 1:1, the student designs and you play a sceptical journalist.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “If I would have more money, I would quit.” > “If I had more money, I would quit.”
  • “Provided that you will work, you get money.” > “Provided that you work, you get money.”
  • “The government should give money for everybody.” > “The government should give money to everybody.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

B. Writing: an essay (280-320 words)

‘A universal basic income is the best response to the rise of automation.’ To what extent do you agree? Refer to evidence from trials and consider at least one alternative policy.

Checklist

  • Use at least one mixed conditional and two alternatives to if, such as provided that, should or had.
  • Use six words from the lesson.
  • Refer to evidence and deal with counterarguments.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

At C1, a strong essay engages with counterarguments and evaluates evidence instead of listing points.
