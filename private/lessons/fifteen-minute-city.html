Everything Within Fifteen Minutes

Should cities be designed so you can walk to everything you need? Learn to negotiate and reach a compromise.

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • make proposals and set conditions
  • reject ideas politely and reach a compromise
  • take part in a multi-person negotiation

Teacher’s overview

Lesson type: Speaking. Length: 75 minutes plus homework.

Main aim: Speaking: negotiating in a multi-party role-play and reaching agreement.

Subsidiary aims: Reading for detail and a model negotiation. Vocabulary for cities and transport.

3

Background

8 min

The 15-Minute City

The idea is simple: everything you need every day, from shops and schools to doctors, parks and work, should be within a fifteen-minute walk or bike ride from home. The concept was popularised by the urban planner Carlos Moreno, and Paris made it part of its plans from 2020, turning streets into bike lanes and school roads into car-free zones.

Supporters say it means cleaner air, less traffic, more time with family and more customers for local businesses. But the idea has also created strong opposition. In Oxford, in the UK, plans for traffic filters, which limit car journeys through some streets at certain times, led to angry protests in 2023.

Some people worried that they would lose the freedom to drive where they wanted. Others said the plans ignored people who live far from the centre or who depend on their cars, such as many disabled people and delivery drivers.

Are the sentences true (T) or false (F)?

  • In a 15-minute city, you can reach daily services quickly without a car.
  • Paris has created more bike lanes.
  • Everyone in Oxford supported the traffic filters.
  • Some critics say the idea ignores people who need cars.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the background text. True or false?

Ask: ‘Would this work in your city? Why or why not?’ One minute only. Save the big discussion for later.

4

A model negotiation

8 min

Station Road

A city planner and a shop owner discuss a plan.

Planner: We’d like to make Station Road car-free at weekends. How would you feel about that?

Shop owner: I’m afraid that’s going to be difficult for us. Most of my customers come by car.

Planner: I understand. What if we added a car park at the end of the road?

Shop owner: That could work, as long as it’s free for the first hour.

Planner: We can’t promise that, but we could offer free parking on Saturday mornings.

Shop owner: OK, I could live with that. And what about deliveries?

Planner: Deliveries would be allowed before 10 a.m. Would that be acceptable?

Shop owner: Yes, that sounds reasonable. Let’s try it for three months and see.

What do they agree on in the end?

  • They agree on...

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the negotiation. What do they agree on?

Two students read it aloud. Ask: ‘Who gave up something? What?’ (Both did. That’s a compromise.)

Teacher notes. 10 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Say: Look at the phrases. Most come from the dialogue. What does each one do?

Board the four functions. Drill the phrases with polite intonation, especially ‘I’m afraid...’ and ‘We can’t promise that, but...’.

Point out: as long as and provided that are followed by the present simple, even about the future.

Speaking: the Riverside plan

22 min

The city wants to make the Riverside neighbourhood a 15-minute neighbourhood. Today, the people affected meet to agree a plan.

The plan

1. Close the main street to cars at weekends. 2. Turn the car park into a park. 3. New bike lanes on all main roads. 4. Traffic filters at school times. 5. A free electric bus every ten minutes.

City planner

You want at least three of the five changes. You have a limited budget, so you can’t do everything.

Café owner

You worry about footfall if cars can’t come. But more pedestrians could also mean more customers.

Elderly resident

You drive to the doctor and the supermarket. You can’t walk far or cycle.

Parent of two

You want safe streets for your children to walk to school and more green space.

Delivery driver

You need access to every street, all day. Traffic filters make your job much harder.

Steps

  1. Read your role. Decide what you want, and what you could accept.
  2. The planner opens the meeting. Everyone gives their position.
  3. Negotiate. Make proposals, set conditions and compromise.
  4. Agree a final package of at least three changes, with any conditions.
  5. Present your agreement to the class.

Useful language

How would you feel about...?What if we...?As long as...I’m afraid I can’t accept...I could live with that, provided that...So, are we all agreed?

Teacher notes. 22 min. Groups of four or five.

Say: You’re at a meeting about the future of Riverside. Read your role. Think about what you want and what you could accept. You have four minutes.

Groups of four or five. 4 min preparation, 13 min negotiation, 5 min presentations.

The planner runs the meeting. Give that role to a confident student.

Monitor for negotiation language. Note which phrases each student used.

One-to-one: the student is the planner. You play two or three other roles in turn.

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘as long as it will be free’ > ‘as long as it is free’
  • ‘What if we will build...?’ > ‘What if we built / build...?’
  • ‘I can agree with it if...’ > ‘I could live with that if...’
