Office: Anywhere

Remote workers are moving abroad with a laptop. Who wins, and who pays?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand an article about remote work and its effect on cities
  • express wishes and regrets with wish and if only
  • see an issue from two sides in a role-play

Background: Since the pandemic, dozens of countries have created visas for remote workers. Cities such as Lisbon, Mexico City and parts of Bali have seen local complaints about rising rents, partly linked to foreign remote workers.

1

Warm-up

6 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. Could you do your job, or your dream job, from a laptop?
  2. If you could work from any city in the world, where would you go? Why?
  3. Do many foreigners live and work remotely in your city? How do locals feel about it?

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Imagine you can work from anywhere. Discuss these questions with your partner.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Yerevan and Tbilisi have large remote-worker communities. Students may have strong local views. Welcome them.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

6 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

Laptop, Passport, Wi-Fi

Dozens of countries now offer special visas to remote workers. For some, it’s a dream life. For local people, it’s more complicated.

Every morning, Elena opens her laptop in a sunny co-working space in Lisbon. She works for a design agency in Toronto, so because of the time zone difference, her working day starts after lunch. In the morning she surfs. ‘Five years ago, I was stuck in an office,’ she says. ‘I used to wish I could work from anywhere. Now I do.’

Elena is one of millions of digital nomads, people who use remote work to live and travel abroad. The pandemic made working from home normal, and many workers realised that ‘home’ could be anywhere with good Wi-Fi. More than fifty countries, from Portugal and Croatia to Georgia and Indonesia, now offer special visas for remote workers.

For governments, the attraction is clear. Nomads earn a foreign income and spend it locally on rent, cafés and tours. But in popular cities, this has a cost. When people with high salaries arrive in a place with a low cost of living, prices rise. In parts of Lisbon, Mexico City and Bali, locals complain about gentrification: rents going up, traditional cafés replaced by expensive brunch spots, and neighbourhoods changing beyond recognition.

The nomad life isn’t always perfect either. Many nomads say they find it hard to integrate. They make friends with other foreigners but rarely with locals, and they move on before they learn the language. ‘Sometimes I feel completely rootless,’ Elena admits. ‘I wish I had a group of friends who had known me for more than three months.’

Some cities are now trying to find a balance, encouraging nomads to stay longer, pay local taxes and take part in community life. And many nomads are rethinking their choices. ‘If only I had learned Portuguese before I came,’ says Elena. ‘I’d understand this city so much better.’

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: B. The text shows the benefits for nomads, the costs for locals and the problems nomads face.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

10 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • Elena works for a company in Lisbon.
  • Elena starts work in the afternoon.
  • Indonesia offers a visa for remote workers.
  • Elena earns more than most people in Lisbon.
  • Nomads usually make many local friends.
  • Elena learned Portuguese before she moved to Lisbon.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “What does not given mean?” (The text doesn’t say.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

12 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • I used to wish I could work from anywhere.
  • I wish I had a group of friends who had known me for more than three months.
  • If only I had learned Portuguese before I came.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Does Elena have a group of long-term friends now?No. wish + past simple = she wants the present to be different.
  • Did Elena learn Portuguese before she came?No. wish / if only + past perfect = regret about the past.
  • What is the difference between wish and if only?The meaning is the same. if only is stronger and more emotional.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: wish and if only.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: wish / if only + past simple = we want a present situation to be different (I wish I had more time). wish + could = ability (I wish I could swim). wish / if only + past perfect = regret about the past (I wish I had studied). wish + would = annoyance about someone else’s behaviour (I wish he would stop).

CCQs: ‘I wish I lived by the sea.’ Do I live by the sea? (No.) Do I want to? (Yes.) ‘I wish I hadn’t moved.’ Did I move? (Yes.) Am I happy about it? (No.)

Common mistake: ‘I wish I have more time.’ After wish, go one step back in time: present becomes past, past becomes past perfect.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

8 min

Complete the sentences with the correct form of the verb in brackets.

  • I hate my commute. I wish I could (can) work from home.
  • I’m so tired. I wish I hadn’t stayed (not stay) up so late last night.
  • If only I spoke (speak) Spanish. I could talk to my neighbours.
  • She wishes she had taken (take) the job in Bali last year.
  • The Wi-Fi here is terrible. I wish it was / were (be) faster.
  • I wish my flatmate would stop (stop) leaving dishes in the sink!

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Is sentence 2 about now or about the past?’ (The past.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

16 min

The nomad and the neighbour

A digital nomad and a local resident meet in a café. They see the city very differently.

The nomad

You moved here four months ago. You love the city. You pay high rent and feel you help the local economy. You’d like local friends, but it’s hard.

The local resident

Your family has lived here for three generations. Your rent went up 40% in two years. Your favourite café is now a co-working space. You don’t dislike foreigners, but you’re frustrated.

Steps

  1. Read your card. Think of two wishes and one regret your character has.
  2. Have a conversation in the café. Introduce yourselves, share your views and listen.
  3. Find two things you could each do to make the situation better.
  4. Step out of your role. Would you like to be a digital nomad? Why or why not?

Useful language

I wish things were...If only the city had...I can see why you feel...From where I stand, ...What would help is...Maybe we could both...

Teacher notes. 16 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: A digital nomad and a local resident meet in a café. Choose a role and read your card. Think of two wishes and one regret. You have three minutes.

Pairs, one role each. 3 min preparation, 7 min conversation, 3 min solutions, 3 min out-of-role discussion.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “I wish I would have more free time.” > “I wish I had more free time.”
  • “I wish I didn’t come here.” > “I wish I hadn’t come here.”
  • “The life costs are very high here.” > “The cost of living is very high here.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

  • It took her a year to integrate into the local community.
  • I rent a desk in a co-working space because I can’t concentrate at home.

B. Writing: a blog post (200-240 words)

Write a blog post called ‘A month as a digital nomad’. Imagine you spent a month working remotely in another country. Describe the good and bad sides, what you wish had been different, and whether you’d do it again.

Checklist

  • Use wish or if only at least three times, about the present and the past.
  • Use five words from the lesson.
  • Write in a personal, engaging style with a clear ending.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

Check the backshift after wish. It is the main source of errors in this lesson.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
