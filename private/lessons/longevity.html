3

First read

7 min

Read the text quickly. Which statement best summarises the article?

Don’t Die, Says the Algorithm

The world’s richest people are trying to buy more time. The science behind their routines is more mixed than their marketing.

Every morning, one of the world’s best-known biohacking enthusiasts, an American tech entrepreneur, wakes before 5 a.m. and begins a regime that is said to cost him around two million dollars a year. He reportedly takes more than a hundred pills a day, eats his last meal before noon and tracks dozens of biomarkers, from blood sugar to the quality of his sleep. His stated goal is simple: to slow down ageing, and perhaps one day to stop it.

He is not alone. Silicon Valley has poured billions into longevity research. Several start-ups are believed to be working on drugs that could reverse cellular ageing, and wealthy clients pay thousands of dollars for full-body scans, personalised supplement plans and experimental therapies. On social media, influencers share morning routines involving ice baths, red-light masks and continuous glucose monitors. It is estimated that the global longevity market is now worth hundreds of billions of dollars.

What does the science actually say? Some research is genuinely exciting. Certain drugs appear to extend the lives of mice and worms, and scientists have identified biological processes that seem to drive ageing. But results in animals often fail to translate to humans, and many popular practices rely on anecdotal evidence rather than clinical trials. A pill that is claimed to ‘turn back the clock’ may simply be an expensive vitamin.

Ironically, the habits with the strongest evidence are the least glamorous. Large studies consistently show that regular exercise, good sleep, a diet rich in vegetables, not smoking and strong social relationships are associated with longer, healthier lives. Many researchers now focus less on lifespan and more on healthspan: the number of years we live free of serious disease. A sedentary millionaire with a cupboard full of supplements may well be worse off than an active grandmother who has never heard of a biomarker.

Critics also raise ethical concerns. If radical life extension ever becomes possible, it is likely to be available first, and perhaps only, to the rich, deepening existing inequalities. Others worry about the psychological cost of an obsessive focus on measuring every heartbeat. Humans have searched for an elixir of eternal youth for thousands of years. The latest version comes with an app, but the most reliable advice is also the oldest: move more, sleep well and look after the people you love.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 4 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: C. Paragraphs 3 and 4 contrast expensive practices with simple, well-supported habits.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

12 min

Read again. Answer the questions in your own words.

  • What makes the entrepreneur’s routine unusual?It is extremely expensive and strict: over a hundred pills a day, no food after noon and constant tracking of dozens of health measurements.
  • Why should we be cautious about results from studies on mice and worms?Results in animals often don’t apply to humans.
  • What does the writer mean by ‘the least glamorous’ habits?Simple, ordinary habits like exercise, sleep and a good diet. They aren’t exciting or fashionable, but they have the strongest evidence.
  • What is the difference between lifespan and healthspan?Lifespan is how long you live. Healthspan is how many of those years you live in good health.
  • What two ethical concerns do critics raise?Life extension might be available only to the rich and increase inequality, and an obsessive focus on measuring health may harm people psychologically.
  • Why does the writer mention the search for an elixir?To show that the dream of living forever is ancient. The technology is new, but the oldest advice is still the most reliable.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Answer the questions in your own words. Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “Can you copy whole sentences from the text?” (No, use your own words.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Good. Notice how careful the writer is with facts. Let’s look at the language that does that.

5

Language focus

14 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • ...a regime that is said to cost him around two million dollars a year.
  • Several start-ups are believed to be working on drugs that could reverse cellular ageing.
  • It is estimated that the global longevity market is now worth hundreds of billions of dollars.
  • Certain drugs appear to extend the lives of mice and worms.
  • A pill that is claimed to ‘turn back the clock’ may simply be an expensive vitamin.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Why does the writer say ‘is said to cost’ instead of ‘costs’?To keep a distance. The writer reports a claim and doesn’t present it as a checked fact.
  • What are the two main structures? Find an example of each.It is + past participle + that clause (It is estimated that...). Subject + passive verb + to-infinitive (is said to cost, are believed to be working).
  • Which example shows an action in progress? How would you show a past action?are believed to be working (to be + -ing). For the past: to have + past participle, e.g. He is thought to have spent...

Teacher notes. 14 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: distancing and impersonal reporting structures.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: Distancing structures report information without full commitment. It is said / thought / believed / estimated / claimed that + clause. Subject + is said / thought / believed + to-infinitive (present), to be + -ing (in progress), to have + past participle (past). Also appear to / seem to + infinitive. They are common in news, academic and formal writing.

CCQs: ‘He is said to have lost his fortune.’ Am I sure he lost it? (No.) Is this about the past or the present? (The past.)

Common mistakes: mixing the two structures (‘He is said that he is rich’) and wrong verb forms (‘is believed to working’). Practise both structures side by side.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

10 min

Rewrite the sentences using the word given. Use a distancing structure.

  • People say that the supplement improves memory.saidThe supplement is said to improve memory. / It is said that the supplement improves memory.
  • Experts believe that ageing is partly genetic.believedAgeing is believed to be partly genetic.
  • People think that the company is developing a new drug.thoughtThe company is thought to be developing a new drug.
  • Reports claim that he spent two million dollars last year.claimedHe is claimed to have spent two million dollars last year.
  • Scientists estimate that one in six people will be over 65 by 2050.estimatedIt is estimated that one in six people will be over 65 by 2050.
  • It seems that regular exercise slows down ageing.appearsRegular exercise appears to slow down ageing.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Does the new sentence sound more certain or less certain?’ (Less certain. More distant.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

20 min

Myth or evidence?

Six popular longevity claims. How strong is the evidence for each one?

Ice baths

An ice bath every morning adds years to your life.

Early eating

Eating your last meal before noon slows down ageing.

Friendships

Strong friendships are as important for health as not smoking.

Body scans

A full-body scan every year keeps healthy people safe.

Walking

Walking about 7,000 steps a day lowers the risk of early death.

Supplements

Expensive supplements can replace a healthy diet.

Steps

  1. For each claim, decide: strong evidence, some evidence or no real evidence. Use distancing language.
  2. Compare with another pair. Where do you disagree?
  3. Discuss: would you want to live to 150? Why or why not?
  4. Agree on a realistic, cheap healthspan plan for an average office worker.

Useful language

This is said to..., but...There appears to be little evidence that...It’s widely believed that...I’d want to see the research.That sounds plausible, but...Frankly, I’d rather...

Teacher notes. 20 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Here are six popular claims about living longer. Decide how strong the evidence is for each one. Use the language of distance: it is said, it appears, it is believed.

Pairs, then groups of four. 3 min sorting, 4 min comparing, 5 min on living to 150, 5 min on the plan. Suggested evidence levels: strong for walking and friendships. Mixed for eating before noon. Little or none for ice baths as a way to live longer, yearly full-body scans for healthy people, and supplements instead of food.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “He is said that he takes 100 pills.” > “He is said to take 100 pills. / It is said that he takes 100 pills.”
  • “It is believed to be true that exercise helps.” > “It is believed that exercise helps.”
  • “I want to live long life.” > “I want to live a long life.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

B. Writing: a proposal (280-320 words)

Your company wants to improve employees’ health. Write a proposal for management. Describe the current situation, suggest three evidence-based measures, and explain the expected benefits and costs.

Checklist

  • Use at least four distancing structures, such as it is believed that and is said to.
  • Use six words from the lesson.
  • Use headings and a formal, persuasive style.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.
