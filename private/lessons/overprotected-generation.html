First read

9 min

Overprotected Offline, Underprotected Online?

1When Jonathan Haidt, a social psychologist at New York University, published The Anxious Generation in 2024, it quickly became one of the most discussed books in the world. Its central claim is simple and provocative: we have overprotected children in the real world and underprotected them online.

2According to Haidt, childhood changed fundamentally in the early 2010s, when smartphones and social media became part of teenage life. At the same time, he argues, anxious parents had already been reducing children’s freedom for decades: fewer children walked to school alone, played outside unsupervised or took small risks, like climbing trees. The result, he claims, is a generation that spends less time in the kind of independent play that builds resilience, and more time on screens that encourage comparison and constant distraction.

3Haidt points to striking data. In several countries, rates of anxiety and depression among teenagers, particularly girls, rose sharply from around 2012. His proposed solutions are equally clear: no smartphones before secondary school, no social media before 16, phone-free schools, and far more unsupervised play and independence in the real world.

4Not all researchers are convinced. Some psychologists argue that the evidence linking social media to poor mental health is largely correlational: it shows that the two trends happened at the same time, not that one caused the other. Several large studies have found only small average effects. Critics also warn that a focus on phones may distract from other possible causes, such as academic pressure, economic insecurity or cuts to mental health services.

5Yet even sceptics tend to agree with part of the argument. Few experts defend the idea that children should spend hours a day on algorithm-driven feeds, and many welcome the call for more outdoor freedom. Some places have even changed the law. In 2018, the US state of Utah passed a ‘free-range parenting’ law, clarifying that letting children walk to school or play outside alone is not, in itself, neglect.

6Perhaps the debate’s greatest contribution is to make parents question two habits at once: hovering over their children in the park, and handing them a device that leaves them alone in a far less predictable world.

Read quickly and answer.

  • What is the article mainly about?
  • Which best describes the writer’s position?

Teacher notes. 9 min. Individual.

Say: Read quickly. What’s the article about, and what is the writer’s position? You have five minutes.

Ask: ‘Which paragraph shows the writer’s own view most clearly?’ (Paragraph 6.)

Say: Strong readers can see how an argument is built. Decide the function of each sentence.

Discuss borderline cases: ‘Fewer children walked to school alone’ is evidence for the claim about lost freedom.

Hedging and stance

12 min

Answer the questions.

  • Paragraph 2 uses ‘he argues’ and ‘he claims’. Why doesn’t the writer say ‘he proves’?To report Haidt’s view without presenting it as established fact. The writer keeps a neutral distance.
  • Find three other words or phrases that show caution or uncertainty.Possible answers: largely, may (distract), possible (causes), tend to, perhaps, some (psychologists).
  • Is ‘rates of anxiety and depression among teenagers rose sharply from around 2012’ a fact or an opinion? How could you check it?It’s a factual claim. You could check official health statistics or the studies cited.
  • Is ‘Perhaps the debate’s greatest contribution is...’ a fact or an opinion? Which words tell you?An opinion. ‘Perhaps’ and ‘greatest’ show evaluation.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs.

Say: Good writers choose their verbs carefully. Answer the questions about how the writer reports and evaluates.

Board a hedging scale: proves > shows > suggests > claims > may. Students will use it in speaking and writing.

Steps

  1. Agree on an age for each milestone. Give reasons.
  2. Use cautious, evidence-based language: the evidence suggests, it could be argued.
  3. Decide: should any of these be laws, or only guidelines?
  4. Present your guidelines. Other groups challenge one age.

Useful language

The evidence suggests...It could be argued that...Correlation isn’t causation.I’d be inclined to say...There’s some truth in that, but...Should that be a law, or just advice?

Teacher notes. 20 min. Groups of three or four.

Say: You’re advising a national parenting organisation. Agree on ages for each milestone. Then decide: law or guideline?

Groups. 3 min reading, 12 min discussing, 5 min presenting and challenging.

Cultural note: expectations differ between countries and families. Welcome different views.

One-to-one: the student sets ages; you represent a very protective parent, then a very relaxed one.

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘This proves that phones cause depression.’ > ‘This suggests a link between phones and depression.’
  • ‘Children must be under control all the time.’ > ‘Children need to be supervised...’
  • ‘The research is correlated.’ > ‘The research is correlational.’
