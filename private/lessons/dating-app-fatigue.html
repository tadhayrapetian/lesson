Swipe Fatigue

Millions of people are deleting their dating apps. Has the algorithm broken romance?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand a complex article about technology and relationships
  • use sentence adverbs to express your attitude precisely
  • discuss sensitive social questions with nuance

Background: Research by Stanford sociologist Michael Rosenfeld found that meeting online had become the most common way for couples in the US to meet. Since the early 2020s, surveys have reported widespread ‘dating app fatigue’, and in-person events such as speed dating and run clubs have grown.

  2. How do most couples meet in your country today?
  3. Would you trust an algorithm to choose a partner for you? Why or why not?

3

First read

7 min

Read the text quickly. What is the writer’s overall view?

Is the Swipe Over?

Dating apps promised to make finding love easy. A decade later, many users are exhausted, and some are going back to old-fashioned ways of meeting.

When dating apps first appeared, they seemed like a miracle. Instead of hoping to meet someone at a party, you could browse hundreds of potential partners from your sofa. In the US, research suggests that meeting online has become the most common way for couples to get together. Yet, ironically, the technology designed to make dating easier has left many users disillusioned. Surveys suggest that a large share of users experience what is now called dating app fatigue: a mix of exhaustion and frustration that makes them delete the apps, sometimes for good.

Part of the problem is the paradox of choice. Psychologists have long argued that having too many options can make us less satisfied with whatever we choose. When an endless supply of profiles is always one swipe away, people may become less committed and more critical, treating potential partners as disposable. Understandably, users who are constantly compared and rejected can start to feel the same way about themselves.

Then there is the behaviour the apps seem to encourage. Many users report being ghosted, when a match suddenly stops replying without explanation. Judging people mainly on a few photos can feel superficial, and arguably it rewards appearance over personality. Critics also point to the business model. Admittedly, apps need to make money, but a company that profits from paid subscriptions has, presumably, less reason to help users find a partner and leave.

Unsurprisingly, alternatives are booming. Speed-dating nights, singles’ hiking trips and run clubs are attracting people who want to meet face to face. Some apps are responding too, with features that encourage fewer, more intentional conversations, or that claim to measure compatibility more deeply than looks. Others are experimenting with AI tools that suggest opening lines or help choose matches.

Will the apps survive? Almost certainly, in some form. For people who are busy, shy or living in small communities, they remain undeniably useful. But many users now combine them with real-world activities, and talk wistfully about serendipity: the chance meeting in a bookshop that no algorithm can plan. Frankly, the lesson may not be that technology has killed romance, but that it cannot do all the work for us.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 4 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: C. The final paragraph: the apps will survive, but they cannot do all the work.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

Close read

12 min

Read again. Answer the questions in your own words.

  • Why does the writer describe the situation as ironic?A technology designed to make dating easier has left many people frustrated and disappointed.
  • How does the paradox of choice affect app users?Endless options make people less committed and more critical, so they treat partners as easy to replace.
  • Why might constant rejection change how users see themselves?If people are always compared and rejected, they may start to feel disposable too.
  • What is the criticism of the subscription business model?A company that earns money from subscriptions may not really want users to find a partner and leave the app.
  • How are some apps responding to user fatigue?With features for fewer, more intentional conversations, deeper compatibility measures and AI tools.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Answer the questions in your own words. Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “Can you copy whole sentences from the text?” (No, use your own words.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Good. This writer is very good at showing an attitude in a single word. Let’s look at how.

Language focus

14 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • Yet, ironically, the technology designed to make dating easier has left many users disillusioned.
  • Understandably, users who are constantly compared and rejected can start to feel the same way about themselves.
  • ...and arguably it rewards appearance over personality.
  • Admittedly, apps need to make money, but...
  • ...a company that profits from paid subscriptions has, presumably, less reason to help users find a partner and leave.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • What does admittedly show about the writer’s attitude?The writer accepts a point against their own argument (a concession) before continuing.
  • Which adverb shows that something can reasonably be claimed, although others may disagree?arguably
  • Which adverbs show that something is logical or expected? Which shows a surprising contrast?understandably and presumably (also unsurprisingly) = logical or expected. ironically = the opposite of what you would expect.

Teacher notes. 14 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: sentence adverbs.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: Sentence adverbs comment on the whole sentence and show the writer’s attitude. Concession: admittedly, granted. Evaluation: arguably, undeniably, frankly, ironically, understandably. Probability: presumably, inevitably, almost certainly. They usually go at the start of a sentence, followed by a comma, or in mid position (after be or an auxiliary, before the main verb).

CCQs: ‘Admittedly, the app is expensive.’ Is the writer happy about the price? (No.) Does the writer accept this point? (Yes, before arguing something else.)

Learners overuse honestly and actually. Push for precise alternatives such as frankly, admittedly and arguably. hopefully (= I hope) is fine in speech but best avoided in formal writing.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

Practice

10 min

Complete the sentences with the sentence adverbs in the box. Use each one once.

inevitablyadmittedlyunderstandablyironicallypresumablyarguably
  • Admittedly, the restaurant was more expensive than we expected, but the food was excellent.
  • She is arguably the best tennis player of her generation, although some fans prefer her rival.
  • Ironically, the fire station burned down.
  • He isn’t answering his phone. He’s presumably still on the plane.
  • After waiting three hours, the passengers were understandably angry.
  • With so many people moving to the city, prices will inevitably rise.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Do these words change the facts or show the writer’s attitude?’ (The attitude.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

20 min

Where do you stand?

Six statements about love in the age of algorithms. Choose three and discuss them in depth.

Family knows best

Introductions by family and friends work better than any app.

The text break-up

It’s acceptable to end a relationship by text message.

The algorithm

Algorithms know what we want better than we do.

First sight

Love at first sight is a myth.

AI messages

Using AI to write your messages to a match is a form of lying.

Silence

Ghosting is sometimes the kindest option.

Steps

  1. Read the six statements. Choose three.
  2. For each statement, give your view. Use at least one sentence adverb each time.
  3. Find the statement your group disagrees on most.
  4. Report to the class: Admittedly, some of us felt..., but...

Useful language

Admittedly, ..., but...Arguably, the real problem is...Frankly, I find that...Understandably, many people...I’d push back on that.There’s a lot to be said for...

Teacher notes. 20 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Here are six statements about modern love. Choose three. Give your opinion on each, and use a sentence adverb every time you do.

Groups of three or four. 2 min reading, 12 min discussion, 4 min reporting. The statement about family introductions often works well with Armenian and Russian-speaking groups. In 1:1, take the opposite view to the student on each statement.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “Honestly, I think that apps are actually bad.” > “Frankly, I think apps do more harm than good.”
  • “He ghosted to me.” > “He ghosted me.”
  • “Arguably that apps are useful.” > “Arguably, apps are useful.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

serendipitysubscriptioncompatibilitysuperficialdisillusioned
  • After years of broken promises, voters became disillusioned with politics.
  • Their compatibility was obvious. They liked the same music, food and jokes.

  • I cancelled my streaming subscription because I never had time to watch anything.

B. Writing: a reply for an advice column (250-300 words)

A reader writes: ‘I’ve been on dating apps for two years. I’m exhausted, and I’m starting to think I’ll never meet anyone.’ Write a reply for an advice column. Be sympathetic, analyse the problem and give practical advice.

Checklist

  • Use at least five different sentence adverbs.
  • Use six words from the lesson.
  • Balance empathy with honest, practical advice.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

Advice columns need a warm but honest voice. Reward precise sentence adverbs over vague ones like honestly and actually.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
