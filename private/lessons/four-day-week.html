The Four-Day Week

Three days off every week. Is this the future of work?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand an article about a work experiment
  • talk about imaginary situations with the second conditional
  • give and defend your opinion in a meeting

Lesson 02

Teacher's overview

Length: 60 minutes plus homework.

Main aim: Speaking: to practise giving and defending opinions in a role-play meeting.

Subsidiary aims: Reading for gist and detail. Vocabulary for work and well-being.

Language aim: Second conditional for imaginary present and future situations.

Background: In 2022, 61 UK companies tried a four-day week for six months with no cut in pay. Most reported stable productivity and fewer staff leaving, and most continued afterwards. Similar trials have run in Iceland, Spain, Japan and other countries.

1

Warm-up

5 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. How many days a week do you, or people in your family, work or study?
  2. What would you do with one extra free day every week?
  3. Do you think people today work too much? Why?

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Look at the three questions. Discuss them with your partner. You have three minutes.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

5 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

Three Days Off Every Week?

Some companies are testing a four-day working week. Is it the future of work?

Imagine finishing work on Thursday afternoon and coming back on Monday. For thousands of workers, this is now normal. In 2022, 61 companies in the UK took part in a six-month trial of the four-day week. Staff worked fewer hours but got the same pay.

The results surprised many people. Most companies said their workers were just as productive as before. Fewer people left their jobs, and fewer took sick days. At the end of the trial, most of the companies decided to continue.

For workers, the biggest benefit is time. ‘If I worked five days again, I wouldn’t see my kids enough,’ says Hana, a designer in Bristol. Many workers said they had less stress and better well-being. Experts say a long weekend can help to prevent burnout.

But the four-day week is not right for every job. Hospitals, schools and shops need people every day. Some workers also found that their workload didn’t change. ‘I have the same work in four days,’ says Tom, an accountant. ‘So my days are longer and more stressful.’

Many employers are now looking for a middle way. Some offer flexible hours or one free Friday a month. ‘If every company changed tomorrow, it would be chaos,’ says one business expert. ‘But slowly, the way we work is changing.’

Teacher notes. 5 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: B. Paragraphs 2 and 3 give good results. Paragraph 4 gives the problems.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

8 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T) or false (F)?

  • Workers in the UK trial earned less money.
  • Most companies kept the four-day week after the trial.
  • Fewer workers took sick days during the trial.
  • Hana wants to go back to a five-day week.
  • Tom’s workload became smaller.
  • Some companies give workers one free Friday a month.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T) or false (F)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “Do you need to show where the answer is?” (Yes.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

10 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • If I worked five days again, I wouldn’t see my kids enough.
  • If every company changed tomorrow, it would be chaos.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Does Hana work five days a week now?No. The situation is imaginary.
  • Which tense comes after if?The past simple: worked, changed.
  • What form is in the other part of the sentence?would / wouldn’t + base verb.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: the second conditional.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: If + past simple, would + base verb. We use it for imaginary or unlikely situations now or in the future. We often say If I were... for all persons. The if-clause can come first or second.

CCQs: ‘If I had a four-day week, I would learn Spanish.’ Do I have a four-day week? (No.) Is this real or imaginary? (Imaginary.) Past or present meaning? (Present.)

Common mistake: ‘If I would have more time...’ We never use would after if in this structure.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

7 min

Complete the second conditional sentences with the verb in brackets.

  • If I had (have) Fridays off, I would go hiking.
  • I would travel (travel) more if I worked only four days.
  • If my boss offered (offer) me a four-day week, I’d say yes immediately.
  • What would you do (you / do) with an extra day off?
  • If we didn’t have (not have) so much work, we could leave early.
  • If I were the manager, I would give (give) everyone flexible hours.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘After if, do we use would?’ (No.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

12 min

The office meeting

You work for a small online shop with 12 workers. Today there is a meeting. Should the company try a four-day week?

The manager

You like the idea, but you worry about customers. They call and email five days a week.

The designer

You are tired and you have two small children. You want more time with your family.

The customer service worker

Customers need answers fast. You think someone must work every weekday.

The HR manager

Many staff feel stressed and three people left last year. You want to keep good workers.

Steps

  1. Choose a role card. Think about what you want and why.
  2. Hold the meeting. Each person gives their opinion.
  3. Find a plan that everyone can accept. Think about rotas, flexible hours and a trial period.
  4. Tell the class your plan.

Useful language

If we had a four-day week, ...The main benefit would be...I’m worried that...What if we tried...?I see your point, but...So, do we all agree on...?

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: You work for a small company. Today there’s a meeting about a four-day week. Choose a role card. You have two minutes to prepare.

Groups of three or four, one role each. In pairs, each student takes two roles. 2 min preparation, 7 min meeting, 3 min reporting.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

3 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 3 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “If I would have a free day, I would sleep.” > “If I had a free day, I would sleep.”
  • “It’s good for the workers health.” > “It’s good for workers’ health.”
  • “I am agree with you.” > “I agree with you.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

flexiblebenefittrialemployerworkload
  • My workload is too big. I have three reports to finish this week.
  • The company is starting a three-month trial of a new app.
  • My employer lets me work from home on Mondays.
  • One benefit of working from home is that you don’t travel.
  • I like jobs with flexible hours, so I can start later.

B. Writing: an email to your manager (100-130 words)

Write an email to your manager. Suggest a four-day week trial for your team. Explain two benefits and answer one possible worry.

Checklist

  • Use the second conditional at least twice.
  • Use four words from the lesson.
  • Start and finish the email politely.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

A good email has a clear purpose in the first line, two benefits, one answer to a worry and a polite ending.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
