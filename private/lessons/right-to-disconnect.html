Off the Clock

New laws give workers the right to ignore their boss after hours. Learn the phrasal verbs and idioms of work-life balance.

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • use phrasal verbs for work and rest
  • use idioms about workload and time
  • discuss what is reasonable at work

2

Read

10 min

The Right to Ignore Your Boss

It’s 9 p.m. You’re winding down on the sofa when your phone buzzes. It’s your manager: ‘Quick question about tomorrow.’ Do you reply?

In Australia, since 2024, many workers have the legal right not to, unless their refusal is unreasonable. Australia is not the first. France introduced a ‘right to disconnect’ in 2017, and Portugal has made it illegal for many employers to contact staff outside working hours.

Supporters say the laws are long overdue. Smartphones mean many people never really log off. They answer emails around the clock, and when they finally switch off, they feel guilty.

Psychologists warn that this constant connection makes it hard to recover from stress. People who are always on call are more likely to burn out. ‘I was snowed under all day, then catching up on emails all night,’ says Mia, a project manager. ‘I felt I had to keep up with everyone, all the time.’

Not everyone is a fan. Some business owners say the rules don’t suit global companies that work across time zones, and that flexible workers sometimes prefer to answer messages in the evening and take time off during the day. Others fear that staff will fall behind if they can’t be contacted.

Most agree on one thing: work-life balance is no longer just a personal problem. It’s becoming a matter of law.

Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • In Australia, workers can always refuse to answer, without exception.
  • France had a right to disconnect before Australia.
  • Mia now works fewer hours.
  • Psychologists link constant connection to burnout.
  • All business owners support the new laws.
  • Some flexible workers like answering messages in the evening.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the article. True, false or not given? You have five minutes.

Students underline every phrasal verb they can find. Who found the most?

6

Speaking: the team contact policy

16 min

Your team is writing a contact policy. When is it OK to message colleagues outside working hours?

10 p.m.

Your manager texts about a meeting at 8 a.m. tomorrow.

7 p.m.

A client in another time zone calls with a question.

Midnight

A colleague sends funny videos in the work chat.

Sunday

Your boss emails: ‘No need to reply until Monday.’

Saturday

The website has crashed during the biggest sale of the year.

Steps

  1. Rank the situations from reasonable to unreasonable.
  2. Agree on five rules for your team’s contact policy.
  3. Present your policy. Other groups can challenge one rule.

Useful language

It’s fine as long as...That’s when people burn out.Nobody should be on call unless...We’d fall behind if...Let’s agree that...That’s a reasonable exception.

Teacher notes. 16 min. Groups of three or four.

Say: Your team needs a contact policy. Look at the five situations. Which are reasonable? Then write five rules.

Groups. 3 min ranking, 8 min writing rules, 5 min presenting and challenging.

Monitor for target phrasal verbs and idioms. Note examples for feedback.

One-to-one: you play a demanding manager. The student negotiates the rules with you.

Reflection and feedback

4 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 4 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘I need to catch up my emails.’ > ‘I need to catch up on my emails.’
  • ‘I was burned.’ > ‘I burned out.’
  • ‘work-life balance is bad’ > ‘I have a poor work-life balance’
9

Homework

Set at the end

A. Rewrite the sentences with a phrasal verb or idiom from the lesson. Use the word given.

  • I have far too much work this week.snowedI’m snowed under this week.
  • Let’s stop working for today.callLet’s call it a day.
  • I need to do the work I missed while I was ill.catchI need to catch up on the work I missed while I was ill.
  • He worked so hard that he became exhausted and ill.burnedHe worked so hard that he burned out.
  • I find it hard to stop thinking about work.switchI find it hard to switch off.

Write a formal email to your manager (150-180 words).

Propose a team agreement about messages after working hours. Explain the problem, suggest three rules and explain the benefits for the team and the company.

Checklist

  • Use at least five phrasal verbs or idioms from the lesson, in a formal enough way.
  • Use a clear structure: reason for writing, proposal, benefits, polite ending.
  • Avoid very informal idioms like ‘beck and call’ in a formal email.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and write the email.

Discuss register: some idioms are too informal for a manager. This is a useful B2 point.
