Worried About the Planet

Climate anxiety is real, especially among young people. A psychologist and a young volunteer explain what helps.

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand a podcast discussion for gist and detail
  • use adjectives of feeling with the correct prepositions
  • give sensitive, practical advice

Teacher’s overview

Lesson type: Listening. Length: 75 minutes plus homework.

Main aim: Listening: gist, specific information and multiple choice in a podcast.

Subsidiary aims: Speaking: giving supportive advice. Vocabulary for emotions and wellbeing.

Language aim: Adjective + preposition: worried about, overwhelmed by, inspired by, focus on.

Background: A 2021 survey of 10,000 people aged 16 to 25 in ten countries, published in The Lancet Planetary Health, found that almost 60% were very or extremely worried about climate change, and about 45% said these feelings affected their daily life.

1

Warm-up

6 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. How do you feel when you read news about the climate?
  2. Do young people worry about the future more than older people? Why?
  3. What do you usually do when you feel worried about something?

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Today’s topic is how people feel about climate change. Discuss the questions with your partner.

Be sensitive. Some students may feel strong anxiety. Keep the focus on general experiences and practical help.

3

Predict

5 min

You will hear a podcast with a psychologist and a 19-year-old climate volunteer. What advice do you think the psychologist will give?

Teacher notes. 5 min. Pairs.

Say: What will the psychologist advise? Write three ideas with your partner.

Keep predictions on the board to check later.

4

First listening

7 min

Mind Matters, podcast
Your teacher will play or read the recording. Listen carefully. You don’t need to understand every word.

Audio script: Mind Matters, podcast. Read it aloud at natural speed, or record it in advance with a colleague.

Nora: Hello and welcome to Mind Matters. Today we’re talking about climate anxiety. With me is Dr Lena Fischer, a psychologist who works with young people, and Arman, a nineteen-year-old student and climate volunteer. Lena, is climate anxiety a mental illness?

Lena: No, and that’s important. Climate anxiety isn’t a disorder. It’s a reasonable response to a real threat. If you look at the news and feel worried, that shows you care. The problem starts when the worry becomes so strong that people can’t sleep, can’t concentrate or feel completely hopeless.

Nora: How common is it?

Lena: Very. A large international survey in 2021 asked ten thousand people aged sixteen to twenty-five in ten countries. Almost sixty per cent said they were very or extremely worried about climate change, and around forty-five per cent said these feelings affected their daily life.

Nora: Arman, does that sound familiar?

Arman: Totally. Two years ago I stopped watching the news. Every headline was about floods or fires, and I felt like nothing I did mattered. I’d lie awake at night thinking, what’s the point of studying if the future is a disaster?

Nora: What changed?

Arman: A friend dragged me to a local tree-planting group. I didn’t expect much, to be honest. But being with other people who cared, and actually doing something with my hands, made a huge difference. The worry didn’t disappear, but it turned into energy.

Lena: That’s exactly what research suggests. Action is one of the best antidotes to anxiety, especially collective action. When you act alone, it’s easy to feel tiny. When you act with others, you feel part of something bigger.

Nora: What advice would you give a listener who feels overwhelmed?

Lena: Three things. First, limit doom-scrolling. Stay informed, but choose a few reliable sources and set a time limit. Second, talk about it. Feelings that you keep inside tend to grow. And third, find one action that matches your skills, whether that’s cycling to work, joining a group or writing to your local politicians.

Arman: Can I add one? Notice the good news too. There’s a lot of progress, like much cheaper solar power, that doesn’t make the headlines.

Nora: Lena, a final word for parents?

Lena: Don’t tell your children not to worry. Listen to them, take their feelings seriously and, if you can, act together.

Listen. Answer the questions. Check your predictions.

  • What is Lena’s main message?
  • How has Arman changed?

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Listen to the podcast. Answer the two questions. Then check your predictions.

Read the script once at natural speed, with a calm voice for Lena and a younger voice for Arman.

5

Second listening

15 min

Listen again. Complete the sentences with one or two words or a number.

  • The 2021 survey asked ten thousand / 10,000 young people.
  • Almost sixty / 60 per cent were very or extremely worried.
  • Arman joined a local tree-planting group.
  • Lena says action is one of the best antidotes to anxiety.

Choose the correct answer.

  • According to Lena, when does climate worry become a problem?
  • Why did Arman stop watching the news?
  • Which is NOT one of Lena’s three tips?
  • What does Lena tell parents?

Teacher notes. 15 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the questions first. Then listen again.

Read the script a second time. Pause after Lena’s three tips so students can check.

In feedback, read the line that gives each answer.

6

Feelings and prepositions

10 min

Complete the sentences with the correct preposition.

  • Many young people are worried about climate change.
  • I sometimes feel overwhelmed by bad news.
  • She’s passionate about protecting the ocean.
  • Don’t feel guilty about taking a break from the news.
  • He was inspired by his friend’s tree-planting group.
  • I feel hopeful about the future when I see young people taking action.
  • We should focus on what we can control.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Feelings often need a preposition. Complete the sentences.

Board pattern: worried / guilty / passionate / hopeful about. overwhelmed / inspired by. focus on.

Common L1 transfer errors: ‘worried for’ (in some uses fine, but not with a topic), ‘inspired from’.

7

Speaking: the advice line

17 min

You work for the podcast’s advice line. Listeners have sent messages. Give each person kind, practical advice.

Message 1

I can’t sleep because of climate news. I lie awake every night.

Message 2

My parents say I’m overreacting. They don’t take me seriously.

Message 3

I want to help, but I’m too busy with exams.

Message 4

I feel guilty every time I take a plane.

Message 5

My friends think climate action is a waste of time.

Steps

  1. Choose three messages.
  2. Discuss how each person feels. Use adjectives with prepositions.
  3. Agree on two pieces of practical advice for each.
  4. Role-play: one of you is the listener, the other gives advice. Swap.

Useful language

It sounds like you’re overwhelmed by...It’s completely normal to feel...Have you thought about...?It might help to focus on...Try not to feel guilty about...You’re not alone.

Teacher notes. 17 min. Pairs.

Say: You work for the advice line. Choose three messages. How does each person feel? What would help?

Pairs. 3 min choosing and planning, 10 min discussing and role-playing, 4 min sharing the best advice.

Model empathetic language first: ‘It sounds like...’, ‘That must be hard.’

One-to-one: you read the messages as the listener. The student gives advice.

8

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘I’m worried for the climate.’ > ‘I’m worried about the climate.’
  • ‘It makes me feel hopeless about.’ > ‘It makes me feel hopeless.’
  • ‘inspired from’ > ‘inspired by’
9

Homework

Set at the end

A. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

lay awakecollectivedoom-scrollingoverwhelmedantidote
  • I felt overwhelmed by all the work I had to do.
  • Laughter is the best antidote to stress.
  • I lay awake until 3 a.m. thinking about the interview.
  • Stop doom-scrolling before bed. It makes you anxious.
  • Change needs collective action, not just individual effort.

Write a reply on an advice page (180-200 words).

A teenager writes: ‘Climate news makes me feel hopeless. What’s the point of anything?’ Write a kind, practical reply. Show that you understand, explain what helps and suggest specific actions.

Checklist

  • Use at least four adjective + preposition combinations.
  • Use five words from the lesson.
  • Be warm and specific. Avoid general phrases like ‘Don’t worry’.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, complete Part A and write the reply.

Mark the reply for tone, organisation and accuracy of prepositions.
