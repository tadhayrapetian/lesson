No One at the Wheel

Driverless taxis are already on the streets of some cities. How likely is it that you’ll ride in one?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • talk about the future with different levels of certainty
  • use be bound to, be likely to, may well, be set to and be unlikely to correctly
  • make and justify predictions about technology

Background: Fully driverless robotaxi services operate in several US cities, including San Francisco and Phoenix, and in Chinese cities such as Wuhan. Safety, weather and jobs remain major questions. The lesson follows a Test-Teach-Test structure.

1

Warm-up

6 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. Would you get into a taxi with no driver? Why or why not?
  2. What jobs do you think machines will do in your city in ten years?
  3. Do you enjoy driving? Would you miss it?

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Imagine a taxi arrives and nobody is driving. Would you get in? Discuss the questions.

2

How much do you know?

6 min

Choose the correct option.

  • Driverless taxis become common in big cities.
  • Prices fall in the next few years.
  • There’s a good chance I’ll try one next year.
  • The company launch its service in June.
  • Accidents happen sometimes.
  • Taxi drivers disappear completely.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual.

Say: Before we start, choose the correct option. Work alone. Don’t worry if you aren’t sure. We’ll check later.

Monitor. Don’t give answers yet.

3

Read

10 min

The Taxi That Drives Itself

Open the app, choose your destination, and a white car arrives. You get in, but the driver’s seat is empty.

In cities such as San Francisco, Phoenix and Wuhan, this is already normal. Driverless ‘robotaxis’ have completed millions of rides, and more cities are set to join the list soon.

Supporters say the technology is bound to make roads safer. Most crashes are caused by human error: drivers who are tired, distracted or drunk. Robots don’t check their phones. Early safety data from some companies looks promising, and costs may well fall as the technology improves.

But not everyone is convinced. Robotaxis have blocked emergency vehicles, stopped in the middle of busy roads and confused traffic police. Snow and heavy rain are still a challenge. Experts say that fully driverless cars are unlikely to work everywhere in the near future, especially in cities with chaotic traffic.

Then there is the question of jobs. Millions of people around the world earn a living by driving. If robotaxis become cheap, many of those jobs could disappear. ‘There’s a good chance my son won’t be a taxi driver like me,’ says Rafael, who has driven a cab for twenty years. ‘Maybe that’s not a bad thing. But what will he do?’

So will you ride in one soon? If you live in a big city, there’s a fair chance you will. If you live in a small town, you’ll probably still need a human for a while.

Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • Robotaxis are already normal in some cities.
  • Supporters say robots are less distracted than human drivers.
  • Robotaxis work well in snow.
  • Rafael is sure his son will become a taxi driver.
  • Robotaxis are cheaper than normal taxis today.
  • The writer thinks small towns will need human drivers for some time.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the article. True, false or not given? You have five minutes.

ICQ: ‘What does not given mean?’ (The text doesn’t say.)

Item 5 is a good NG trap: the text talks about costs falling in the future, not today’s prices.

4

Language focus

14 min

Look at these examples.

  • ...more cities are set to join the list soon.
  • ...the technology is bound to make roads safer.
  • ...costs may well fall as the technology improves.
  • ...fully driverless cars are unlikely to work everywhere in the near future...
  • There’s a good chance my son won’t be a taxi driver like me.
  • ...you’ll probably still need a human for a while.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Put these on a scale from certain to very improbable: bound to, likely to, may well, could, unlikely to.bound to (almost certain), likely to / may well (probable), could (possible), unlikely to (improbable).
  • What follows be bound to, be likely to and be set to? What follows may well?be bound / likely / set to + infinitive. may well + base verb, without to.
  • Where does probably go with will and with won’t?After will: will probably. Before won’t: probably won’t.

Rule for the board: future probability

Certain: will definitely, be bound to + infinitive. Probable: will probably, be likely to, may well, there’s a good chance (that). Possible: could, might, may. Improbable: be unlikely to, probably won’t, there’s little chance (that). Planned or officially expected: be set to, be due to + infinitive. Word order: will definitely / probably, but definitely / probably won’t.

CCQs: ‘Prices are bound to rise.’ How sure am I? (Almost certain.) ‘Prices may well rise.’ (Probable, not certain.) ‘The service is set to start in May.’ Is this a plan? (Yes.)

Common mistakes: ‘is likely become’ (missing to), ‘may well to fall’ (extra to), and ‘won’t probably’. Also, Russian speakers often translate ‘наверное’ as ‘maybe’ in every sentence. Show the range of options.

Teacher notes. 14 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Answer the questions with your partner.

Draw a certainty line on the board from 0% to 100%. Students place the expressions on it.

Say: Now go back to stage 2. Check and correct your answers.

Students self-correct the test. Confirm with the key.

Practice: complete

8 min

Complete the sentences with the words in the box. Use each one once.

chancewellboundunlikelylikelyset
  • Look at those clouds. It’s bound to rain.
  • The new metro line is set to open next spring, the city says.
  • Prices may well go down next year.
  • There’s a good chance that I’ll buy an electric car.

ICQ: ‘How many times can you use each word?’ (Once.)

6

Practice: rewrite

8 min

Rewrite the sentences with the word given. Keep the meaning.

  • I’m sure the company will make mistakes.boundThe company is bound to make mistakes.
  • It’s probable that taxis will get cheaper.likelyTaxis are likely to get cheaper.
  • The service will officially start in May.setThe service is set to start in May.
  • I don’t think he’ll pass his driving test.unlikelyHe is unlikely to pass his driving test.
  • Perhaps they’ll change the law. It’s quite probable.wellThey may well change the law.
  • It’s probable that I’ll take the train.chanceThere’s a good chance (that) I’ll take the train.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Rewrite the sentences using the word given. Think about the level of certainty.

In feedback, ask ‘How sure is the speaker?’ for each item.

7

Practice: your predictions

8 min

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Complete the sentences so they are true for you. Then compare with your partner. Ask ‘Why?’

Students share the most surprising prediction with the class.

8

Speaking: the 2040 panel

12 min

You are a panel of experts. Predict transport in your city in 2040.

Driverless taxis

Will they be everywhere, or only in some areas?

Flying taxis and drones

Science fiction, or the next big thing?

Cars in the city centre

Will private cars be banned from city centres?

Public transport

Better, cheaper, free?

Driving licences

Will teenagers still learn to drive?

Steps

  1. For each card, agree on a prediction and a level of certainty.
  2. Give a reason for each prediction.
  3. Compare with another group. Find your biggest disagreement.
  4. Present your most confident and your least confident prediction.

Useful language

It’s bound to happen because...There’s a good chance that...It may well...I think it’s unlikely to... because...I’d say it’s about 50/50.I’m not convinced.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Groups of three or four.

Say: You’re a panel of transport experts. Make predictions for 2040. Agree on how certain you are.

Groups. 2 min reading, 6 min discussing, 4 min comparing. Monitor for range: push students beyond ‘will’ and ‘maybe’.

One-to-one: the student makes predictions; you challenge each one with ‘How sure are you? Why?’

9

Reflection and feedback

3 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 3 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘Prices are likely fall.’ > ‘Prices are likely to fall.’
  • ‘They may well to arrive late.’ > ‘They may well arrive late.’
  • ‘He won’t probably come.’ > ‘He probably won’t come.’
10

Homework

Set at the end

A. Each sentence has one mistake. Write the correct sentence.

  • Prices are likely fall next year.Prices are likely to fall next year.
  • They may well to arrive late.They may well arrive late.
  • He won’t probably come.He probably won’t come.
  • There’s a good chance of I’ll go.There’s a good chance (that) I’ll go.
  • The concert is set start at eight.The concert is set to start at eight.

Write an article (150-180 words).

Write an article for a city website: ‘How we will travel in my city in 2040’. Make predictions with different levels of certainty and give reasons.

Checklist

  • Use at least five different expressions of probability.
  • Include at least one improbable prediction.
  • Use paragraphs and a clear conclusion.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, correct the sentences and write the article.

Mark the article for range of probability language, not only accuracy.
