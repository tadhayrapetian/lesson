Tickets to Orbit

Space travel is no longer only for astronauts. Is it a giant leap, or a billionaire’s joyride?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand a complex article about space, business and ethics
  • use participle clauses to write concisely
  • debate priorities and justify a difficult choice

Warm-up

7 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. Would you go to space if someone gave you a free ticket? Why or why not?
  2. What do you imagine you would feel, looking down at the Earth?
  3. Should space exploration be done by governments or by private companies?

Teacher notes. 7 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Imagine someone offers you a free ticket to space. Discuss these questions with your partner.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

7 min

Read the text quickly. Which statement best describes the structure of the article?

Eleven Minutes in Space

Private rockets now carry paying passengers to the edge of space. Supporters call it the start of a new era. Critics call it the most expensive selfie in history.

Strapped into a small capsule in the Texas desert, six passengers wait for the countdown to end. Eleven minutes later, having crossed the boundary of space and floated briefly in weightlessness, they are back on the ground, waving at cameras. This is space tourism today: short suborbital flights, sold by private companies to anyone who can afford a ticket costing hundreds of thousands of dollars.

Since 2021, companies founded by some of the world’s richest men have commercialised what was once reserved for government astronauts. Passengers have included actors, singers and entrepreneurs, as well as a few people whose seats were paid for by sponsors. In April 2025, a flight carrying an all-female crew, including a pop star, attracted huge media attention, along with a wave of criticism.

Supporters see these flights as the first steps towards a new frontier. Built on decades of public research, the private space industry has already reduced the cost of launching satellites, and its supporters argue that tourism revenue will fund more ambitious projects, from orbital hotels to missions to Mars. Many astronauts also describe the ‘overview effect’: seeing the Earth as a fragile blue ball with no visible borders permanently changes how they think about humanity and the planet. If more people could experience it, the argument goes, we might take better care of our world.

Critics are less romantic. Each flight, they point out, has a carbon footprint per passenger far larger than that of a long-haul flight, and rocket emissions released high in the atmosphere may have effects that are not yet fully understood. At a time of climate crisis and rising inequality, many see these trips as an exclusive joyride for billionaires and their friends, a symbol of everything that is wrong with how wealth is spent.

There are also practical questions. Who should regulate private spaceflight, and how safe is safe enough? Space is getting crowded, too, with thousands of commercial satellites now circling the planet. Defenders reply that every new technology, from aviation to the internet, began as a luxury before becoming accessible, and that spin-offs from space research have given us everything from memory foam to better water filters. Looking back in fifty years, will we see this era as the dawn of space travel for all, or as an expensive distraction from problems here on Earth?

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 4 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: A. Paragraph 1 describes a flight. Paragraphs 3 to 5 give both sides. The last sentence is an open question.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

5

Language focus

14 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • Strapped into a small capsule in the Texas desert, six passengers wait for the countdown to end.
  • Eleven minutes later, having crossed the boundary of space and floated briefly in weightlessness, they are back on the ground.
  • ...a flight carrying an all-female crew...
  • Built on decades of public research, the private space industry has already reduced the cost of launching satellites.
  • ...rocket emissions released high in the atmosphere...

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Which participle clauses have an active meaning, and which have a passive meaning?Active: carrying, having crossed. Passive: strapped, built, released.
  • Rewrite ‘a flight carrying an all-female crew’ and ‘emissions released high in the atmosphere’ with relative pronouns.a flight which carried / was carrying an all-female crew. emissions which are released high in the atmosphere.
  • What does ‘having crossed’ show about the order of events?The crossing happened before the main action. A perfect participle shows an earlier action.

Teacher notes. 14 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: participle clauses.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: Participle clauses make writing shorter and more formal. Present participle (-ing) = active meaning (a flight carrying..., Looking back...). Past participle = passive meaning (Built on..., emissions released...). Perfect participle (having + past participle) = an action completed before the main action. The participle and the main clause must have the same subject, or the result is a dangling participle.

CCQs: ‘Having finished the report, she went home.’ What happened first? (She finished the report.) Who finished it? (She did.)

Dangling participles: ‘Walking to work, the rain started.’ The rain wasn’t walking. Correct: ‘Walking to work, I got caught in the rain.’

Say: Now let’s practise it.

Practice

10 min

Rewrite the sentences with a participle clause, using the word given.

  • The rocket was designed in Texas. It can carry six people.DesignedDesigned in Texas, the rocket can carry six people.
  • She had trained for months. She felt ready for the flight.HavingHaving trained for months, she felt ready for the flight.
  • Passengers who travel to space must pass a medical check.travellingPassengers travelling to space must pass a medical check.
  • As he looked down at the Earth, he started to cry.LookingLooking down at the Earth, he started to cry.
  • Satellites that were launched in the 1990s are still circling the planet.launchedSatellites launched in the 1990s are still circling the planet.
  • After the company had sold all the tickets, it announced a second flight.HavingHaving sold all the tickets, the company announced a second flight.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Does the new sentence need who or which?’ (No.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

20 min

Who gets the seat?

A space company is giving away one free seat on its next flight. Six people have applied. You are the selection panel.

A climate scientist, 52

Wants to see the atmosphere from above for her research and to share it with the public.

A school student, 17

Won a national science competition. Dreams of becoming an engineer.

An influencer, 29

Has 40 million followers. Promises to make science exciting for young people.

A retired teacher, 74

Has dreamed of space since watching the Moon landing as a child.

A disabled athlete, 35

Wants to prove that space should be open to everyone.

A poet, 41

Wants to write about the overview effect for people who will never go.

Steps

  1. Read the six applications.
  2. Discuss the strongest argument for each person.
  3. Choose one winner and one reserve.
  4. Discuss: should space tourism exist at all? What rules would you introduce?

Useful language

Having heard all the arguments, I’d...Given her background, ...Chosen for the right reasons, the winner could...The case for him is compelling, but...I’m torn between... and...On balance, ...

Teacher notes. 20 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: A space company is giving away one free seat. You’re the selection panel. Read the six applications and choose a winner and a reserve.

Groups of three or four. 3 min reading, 10 min discussion, 3 min announcing the winners, 4 min on the final question. In 1:1, the student decides and you argue for the candidates they reject.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “Arriving at the station, the train had left.” > “When we arrived at the station, the train had already left.”
  • “Being very expensive, I didn’t buy a ticket.” > “Because it was very expensive, I didn’t buy a ticket.”
  • “Cosmos tourism is developing fast.” > “Space tourism is developing fast.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

B. Writing: an opinion article (280-320 words)

Write an opinion article for an online magazine: ‘Space tourism: giant leap or billionaire’s joyride?’ Present both sides and argue for your own view.

Checklist

  • Use at least four participle clauses: present, past and perfect.
  • Use six words from the lesson.
  • Hook the reader in the first paragraph and end with a memorable conclusion.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

Check for dangling participles. They are the most common error with this structure.
