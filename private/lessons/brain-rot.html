3

First read

7 min

Read the text quickly. Which statement best summarises the writer’s position?

Is Your Brain Really Rotting?

Oxford’s Word of the Year for 2024 named a very modern fear. But what does the science actually say?

In December 2024, Oxford University Press announced its Word of the Year: ‘brain rot’. The term describes the supposed deterioration of a person’s mental state caused by consuming too much trivial, low-effort online content. Its use rose by around 230 per cent in a single year. What makes the choice remarkable, though, is that the phrase was not coined by tech critics. It first appeared in 1854, in Henry David Thoreau’s Walden, where he complained that society valued simple ideas over complex ones.

Today, it is young people themselves who use the term most, often ironically. Teenagers describe nonsense memes as ‘brain rot’ and joke that hours of compulsive scrolling have rotted their minds. This self-aware humour may explain why the word resonated so widely. It names a feeling many people recognise: that their attention has been broken into pieces by endless short videos.

But is there real evidence of harm? The research is more complicated than the headlines suggest. Some studies link heavy social media use to poorer concentration, sleep problems and lower wellbeing, particularly among adolescents. Others find only small effects, or suggest that the relationship works both ways: people who are already anxious or bored may scroll more, rather than scrolling causing the problem. What researchers do agree on is that the type of use matters more than the total amount. Passive consumption seems more harmful than active, social use.

Some academics warn that the current alarm resembles a moral panic. Novels, radio, comic books and television were all once accused of damaging young minds. It wasn’t the technology that ruined those generations, they point out. It was adults who failed to understand it. Others reply that this time really is different, because apps are deliberately engineered to maximise engagement, using the same psychological tricks as slot machines.

The truth probably lies in the nuance. Short videos are not poison, but neither are they cognitively harmless when consumed for hours with no purpose. All that most experts recommend is balance: protecting time for deep reading, real conversation and boredom, which is, ironically, where many of our best ideas begin.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 4 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: B. Paragraph 5 states the position directly: ‘the truth probably lies in the nuance’.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

Language focus

14 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • What makes the choice remarkable, though, is that the phrase was not coined by tech critics.
  • Today, it is young people themselves who use the term most.
  • What researchers do agree on is that the type of use matters.
  • It wasn’t the technology that ruined those generations.
  • All that most experts recommend is balance.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Rewrite ‘It is young people themselves who use the term most’ as a simple sentence. What does the cleft version add?Young people themselves use the term most. The cleft version puts the focus on young people and contrasts them with other groups.
  • In ‘What researchers do agree on is that...’, which part carries the key information?The part after is: that the type of use matters more than the amount.
  • What does ‘All that most experts recommend is balance’ emphasise?That the advice is simple and limited: only balance, nothing extreme.

Teacher notes. 14 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: cleft sentences.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: Cleft sentences split one idea into two clauses to focus attention. It is / was + focus + that / who + rest (It was Thoreau who first used it). What + clause + is / was + focus (What I need is time). All (that) + clause + is = only this. The thing / reason / place + clause + is... Use them for emphasis, contrast and correction. Auxiliary do adds extra emphasis: What they do agree on is...

CCQs: ‘It was Ana who called, not Maria.’ Did Maria call? (No.) Why use this structure? (To correct or contrast.)

Learners often drop the second be: ‘What I need more time.’ The verb after a what-clause is usually singular: ‘What we need is more teachers.’

Say: Now let’s practise it.

7

Speaking

20 min

Moral panic or real threat?

Every generation has feared a new medium. Is short-form video just the latest example, or something genuinely new?

1850s: Novels

Critics claimed novels made young readers unable to tell fantasy from reality.

1930s: Radio

Radio dramas were blamed for frightening children and distracting them from homework.

1950s: Comic books

In the US, comics were blamed for youth crime and taken to a Senate hearing.

1990s: Video games

Video games were linked to violence and social isolation.

2020s: Short-form video

Endless short clips are blamed for shrinking attention spans.

Steps

  1. Read the cards. Which fears now seem exaggerated? Which seem justified?
  2. Is short-form video different from these earlier media? If so, what is it that makes it different?
  3. Place yourself on a line from ‘pure moral panic’ to ‘serious threat’. Justify your position.
  4. Agree on three realistic recommendations: one for a teenager, one for a parent and one for a school.

Useful language

What concerns me most is...It’s not the medium itself that...The difference this time is...I’d take issue with that.That’s a slightly simplistic view.On balance, I’d say...

Teacher notes. 20 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Here are five media panics from history. Read them. Which fears now look silly? Which were justified? Then we’ll ask: is today different?

Groups of three or four. 3 min reading, 8 min discussion, 4 min position line, 5 min recommendations. In 1:1, use a scale from 1 to 10 instead of a line.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “What I need it is more sleep.” > “What I need is more sleep.”
  • “It was my brother who he showed me.” > “It was my brother who showed me.”
  • “This content makes damage to the brain.” > “This content can damage the brain.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

nuancecoineddeteriorationresonatedtrivial

  • Don’t waste your time on trivial arguments online.

  • Doctors noticed a gradual deterioration in his health.

B. Writing: a discursive essay (250-300 words)

‘Concern about short-form video is just the latest moral panic.’ Discuss. Consider the evidence on both sides and reach a reasoned conclusion.

Checklist

  • Use at least three different cleft structures.
  • Use six words from the lesson.
  • Support each main point with an example or evidence.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

Look for real argument: claims supported by evidence, and a conclusion that follows from the discussion.
