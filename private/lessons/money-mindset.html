Money Mindset: Why We Buy What We Don't Need

We like to think our spending is rational — it rarely is. Today we dig into the guilt, the habits inherited from our parents, and the hypothetical lives we imagine every time we say "if I had the money…"

1

Warm-Up: Talking Points

Before we open a single book, talk to a partner. Be honest — there are no wrong answers here.

  • 1.Do you think the way you handle money says something about who you are as a person, or is it just a practical skill, unrelated to identity?
  • 2.Have you ever felt guilty about spending money on something you actually needed? Where do you think that guilt comes from?
  • 3.Which financial habit did you inherit from your parents or family — and is it one you'd actually like to change?
  • 4.Is it possible to buy your way into feeling better after a bad day? Does it ever actually work, in your experience?

Grammar Focus: Second Conditional

Form

We use the second conditional to talk about unreal, imagined or unlikely situations in the present or future — and their imagined results. It's the language of "what if," daydreaming, and hypothetical honesty about money.

If + past simple, ... would + base verb

The "if clause" describes an imaginary condition; the main clause describes its imagined consequence. The clauses can also be reversed: I would feel less anxious if I had savings.

First vs. Second Conditional — Real vs. Unreal
First Conditional (real / likely) Second Conditional (unreal / hypothetical)
Form If + present simple, will + base verb If + past simple, would + base verb
Meaning A realistic possibility An imaginary or unlikely situation
Example If I save $50 this month, I'll finally buy that jacket. If I had $50,000, I would pay off every debt I have.

Note: In formal or careful speech, many speakers use were instead of was for all subjects: If I were less impulsive, I would save more.

Sophisticated Examples

  • If I had more disposable income, I wouldn't feel the need to justify every small splurge.
  • If she weren't so afraid of financial anxiety, she would actually look at her bank statements.
  • My habits would probably be completely different if my parents had talked openly about money.
  • If he stopped comparing his life to what he sees online, he wouldn't engage in so much revenge spending.
  • What would you do differently if nobody could ever judge your spending?

Controlled Practice

Complete the exercises below. Some are multiple choice, some are gap-fills, and some ask you to rewrite a sentence.

1.If I ______ (have) more free time, I would think less about shopping online.

2.If she ______ (not / feel) guilty about spending on herself, she would enjoy her purchases far more.

3.If we lived within our means, we ______ in debt right now.

4.Rewrite using the Second Conditional: "I don't have enough savings, so I can't quit this job."

5.If he ______ (not / spend) so much money eating out, he would have a healthier savings account.

6.What would you do if you ______ (win) a large sum of money tomorrow?

7.Rewrite using the Second Conditional: "I'm not frugal, so I overspend on clothes I don't need."

8.If my grandmother ______ (see) my monthly coffee spending, she would be genuinely shocked.

9.If they ______ (stop) comparing themselves to their coworkers, they would feel far less pressure to splurge.

10.Complete with your own true ideas: "If I weren't so ______ (adjective) about money, I would ______."

6

Production Task: Long Turn

Prepare a short talk (2–3 minutes) on the topic: "My money mindset — and the one I wish I had." Use the guiding points below, and try to use at least three vocabulary items and three second conditional sentences.

  • Describe your own spending personality — are you more of a saver, a splurger, or somewhere in between?
  • Explain one financial habit you learned from your family, and whether it still serves you today.
  • Use the second conditional to describe how your life would be different with a completely different money mindset.
  • Talk about a moment of buyer's remorse or revenge spending you (or someone you know) has experienced.
  • Finish with what you would change first, if you could change only one financial habit.
