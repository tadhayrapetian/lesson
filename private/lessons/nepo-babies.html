Born Into It

From Hollywood to family firms, ‘nepo babies’ have sparked a debate about talent, luck and fairness.

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • use the unreal past to talk about preferences, criticism and imaginary situations
  • use it’s high time, would rather, would sooner, as if and supposing accurately
  • discuss fairness and privilege with nuance

3

Read

12 min

The Nepo Baby Debate

In late 2022, a magazine cover declared it ‘the year of the nepo baby’, and the phrase exploded online. Short for ‘nepotism baby’, it describes someone whose career seems to have been helped by famous or well-connected parents: the actor whose mother is a film star, the model whose father owns an agency.

Critics say it’s high time the entertainment industry admitted the problem. Auditions are supposed to be fair, they argue, but when a famous surname opens doors, talented outsiders never get a chance. ‘They talk as though they had got there entirely on their own,’ complains one casting director, ‘but their first role came from a family friend.’

Many so-called nepo babies find the label unfair. A famous name might get you into the room, they argue, but it won’t keep you there. ‘I’d rather people judged my work than my parents,’ one young actor said in an interview. Others point out that children follow their parents into many careers, from medicine to farming, and nobody complains when a carpenter’s daughter becomes a carpenter.

The debate goes beyond Hollywood. Sociologists note that in many professions, from law to journalism, people from wealthy families are over-represented. Unpaid internships, which only those with financial support can afford, and personal networks often matter as much as qualifications. Supposing two candidates had identical CVs, the one with the right contacts would usually still win.

So what should change? Some companies now hide names in the first round of applications, and some industries are replacing unpaid internships with paid ones. Perhaps the real question isn’t whether nepo babies deserve their success, but whether everyone else gets a fair chance to compete. As one commentator put it, ‘I’d sooner we fixed the system than blamed the children of the people who built it.’

Answer the questions in your own words.

  • What does the term ‘nepo baby’ mean?Someone whose career seems to have been helped by famous or well-connected parents.
  • What is the critics’ main argument in paragraph 2?Famous surnames give unfair access, so talented outsiders miss out, and nepo babies pretend they succeeded alone.
  • How do some ‘nepo babies’ defend themselves?A name may open the door, but talent keeps you there. And children follow their parents into many careers without criticism.
  • What wider point do sociologists make?Advantage exists in many professions, through money, unpaid internships and networks, not only in Hollywood.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the article. Answer the questions in your own words.

Ask: ‘Whose argument do you find most convincing?’ Keep it brief.

Language focus

15 min

Look at these examples.

  • Critics say it’s high time the entertainment industry admitted the problem.
  • They talk as though they had got there entirely on their own.
  • I’d rather people judged my work than my parents.
  • Supposing two candidates had identical CVs, the one with the right contacts would usually still win.
  • I’d sooner we fixed the system than blamed the children of the people who built it.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Has the industry admitted the problem? Which tense follows it’s high time, and why?No. The past simple after it’s (high / about) time shows something should already be happening. It refers to now, not the past.
  • Compare ‘I’d rather judge’ and ‘I’d rather people judged’. When do we use a past form after would rather?When the subject changes: would rather + another person + past simple. Same subject: would rather + base verb.
  • In ‘as though they had got there on their own’, is it true? Why the past perfect?No, the speaker doesn’t believe it. The past perfect shows an unreal situation about the past.

Rule for the board: the unreal past

A past form can show that something is hypothetical, not real. it’s (high / about) time + subject + past simple = it should happen now. would rather / would sooner + subject + past simple = preference about another person’s actions (I’d rather you stayed). Same subject: would rather + base verb. Past regret: would rather + subject + past perfect (I’d rather you hadn’t told him). as if / as though + past simple or past perfect = unreal comparison. When the comparison may be true, use present forms (You look as if you’re tired). suppose / supposing + past = imagine.

CCQs: ‘It’s time we left.’ Have we left? (No.) Is this about the past? (No, now.) ‘I’d rather you hadn’t invited him.’ Did you invite him? (Yes.) Am I happy? (No.)

Common mistakes: ‘It’s time we go’ (went, or It’s time to go), ‘I’d rather you don’t’ (didn’t), ‘I’d rather to walk’ (no to).

Teacher notes. 15 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Answer the questions with your partner.

Elicit the key idea: here, past forms don’t mean past time. They mean ‘not real’. Draw two columns on the board: form and time.

Say: Now go back to stage 2. Check and correct your answers.

Practice: forms

9 min

Complete the sentences with the correct form of the verb in brackets.

  • It’s high time you started (start) applying for jobs.
  • I’d rather you didn’t mention (not mention) my parents in the interview.
  • She acts as if she owned (own) the place.
  • I’d rather stay (stay) at home tonight, if you don’t mind.
  • Supposing you inherited (inherit) a million dollars, what would you do?
  • I’d rather you hadn’t told (not tell) him yesterday. Now everyone knows.
  • It’s about time the government did (do) something about unpaid internships.

Teacher notes. 9 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Complete the sentences. Think: is the subject the same or different? Is it about now or about the past?

Feedback: ask students to explain items 4 and 6.

Speaking: fair or unfair?

15 min

The family firm

A CEO makes her son, 24, head of marketing.

The internship

A politician’s daughter gets an internship at a top newspaper.

The audition

A famous actor’s son wins a role after one audition.

The farm

A farmer leaves the family farm to his daughter.

The referral

You get a job because a friend recommended you.

Steps

  1. Rank the situations from fair to unfair.
  2. Discuss what makes the difference: money, talent, transparency, competition?
  3. Agree on three changes that would make access to good jobs fairer.
  4. Present your changes. Use at least three structures from the lesson.

Useful language

It’s high time companies...I’d rather they...It’s as if...Supposing the son had no experience, ...To my mind, the key difference is...That’s a slightly different case, though.

Teacher notes. 15 min. Groups of three or four.

Say: Look at the five situations. Rank them from fair to unfair. Then agree on three changes.

Groups. 3 min reading, 8 min discussion, 4 min presenting.

Push for nuance at C1: ‘It depends on...’, ‘The key difference is...’.

One-to-one: take the opposite view on each situation.

9

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘It’s time we go.’ > ‘It’s time we went.’
  • ‘I’d rather you don’t do that.’ > ‘I’d rather you didn’t do that.’
  • ‘He speaks as if he knows everything.’ Fine if it may be true. ‘as if he knew’ if we think it’s not true.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Each sentence has one mistake. Write the correct sentence.

  • It’s time we go home.It’s time we went home. / It’s time to go home.
  • I’d rather you don’t smoke here.I’d rather you didn’t smoke here.
  • I’d rather to walk.I’d rather walk.
  • He spoke as if he knows everything, but he was wrong.He spoke as if he knew everything, but he was wrong.

Write an opinion article (250-280 words).

Write an article for an online magazine: ‘Is it wrong for parents to use their connections to help their children?’ Present both sides and give your own reasoned view.

Checklist

  • Use at least four structures from the lesson.
  • Support your points with examples.
  • End with a memorable final sentence.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, correct the sentences and write the article.

Mark for accuracy of the unreal past and for the quality of argument.
