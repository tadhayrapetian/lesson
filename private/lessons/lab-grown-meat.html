Meat Without the Animal

Cultivated meat is on a few menus already. Would you eat it?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand an article about food technology
  • use the future continuous and the future perfect to make predictions
  • argue for or against an idea in a structured debate

1

Warm-up

6 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. How often do you eat meat? Could you stop?
  2. Would you eat a burger grown in a lab? Why or why not?
  3. What will people eat in 2050, do you think?

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Let’s talk about the food of the future. Discuss these questions with your partner.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

6 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

Steak from a Lab

It looks like meat, it tastes like meat, and it is meat. But no animal died to make it.

Imagine a burger made from real beef, but without a cow. That’s the promise of cultivated meat. Scientists take a small number of cells from a living animal and grow them in large steel tanks, feeding them nutrients until they become meat. The process takes weeks, not years, and the animal is unharmed.

The idea is no longer science fiction. In 2020, Singapore became the first country to approve the sale of cultivated chicken. The US followed in 2023, and a few restaurants started serving it. Supporters say that by 2040, millions of people will be eating meat grown in this way.

Why is this important? Raising livestock uses huge amounts of land and water, and it is responsible for a significant share of global greenhouse gas emissions. Unlike plant-based burgers, which try to copy meat, cultivated meat is real meat, so people wouldn’t have to give up the taste they love.

But there are big challenges. Producing cultivated meat is still very expensive, and companies are struggling to scale up. Some scientists doubt it will ever be as cheap as farm meat. Many consumers are sceptical too. In surveys, people often describe it as unnatural, or say they would not feel comfortable eating it.

Politics has entered the debate. In 2023, Italy decided to ban the production and sale of cultivated meat, saying it wanted to protect traditional farming and food culture. Florida and Alabama passed similar bans in 2024. ‘By the end of this decade, we will have learned whether this is a revolution or an expensive experiment,’ says one food scientist. ‘The next few years will be decisive.’

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: B. Benefits in paragraph 3, problems in paragraphs 4 and 5.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

10 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • Animals are killed to produce cultivated meat.
  • Singapore approved cultivated meat before the US.
  • Cultivated meat tastes better than farm meat.
  • Plant-based burgers are made from real meat.
  • All scientists believe cultivated meat will become cheap.
  • Italy banned cultivated meat partly to protect its farmers.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “What does not given mean?” (The text doesn’t say.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

12 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • Supporters say that by 2040, millions of people will be eating meat grown in this way.
  • By the end of this decade, we will have learned whether this is a revolution.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Which sentence describes an action in progress at a time in the future?will be eating (future continuous).
  • Which sentence describes an action completed before a time in the future?will have learned (future perfect).
  • Which preposition often goes with the future perfect?by: by 2030, by the end of the decade.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: future continuous and future perfect.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: Future continuous: will be + -ing = an action in progress at a point in the future. Future perfect: will have + past participle = an action completed before a point in the future. by + time = not later than.

CCQs: ‘This time tomorrow, I’ll be flying to Paris.’ At that time, will I be in the air? (Yes.) ‘By Friday, I’ll have finished the report.’ Will it be finished on Friday or earlier? (Yes, on Friday or before.)

Students often say ‘By 2030 prices will fall’ when they mean completion before that time. Draw a timeline on the board to contrast the two forms.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

8 min

Complete the sentences with the future continuous or the future perfect of the verb in brackets.

  • By 2035, many countries will have approved (approve) cultivated meat.
  • This time next year, I will be studying (study) food science in Copenhagen.
  • Don’t call me at 8. We will be having (have) dinner.
  • By the time you arrive, the chef will have cooked (cook) everything.
  • In twenty years, people will still be eating (still / eat) farm meat. I’m sure.
  • By the end of the week, the company will have produced (produce) 1,000 kg of chicken.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Which form usually goes with by?’ (The future perfect.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

16 min

The great food debate

The motion: ‘In 2040, cultivated meat should replace most farm meat.’ Choose a side and argue it.

Environment

Land, water and emissions.

Animal welfare

Billions of animals are farmed every year.

Health and safety

Is it safe? What goes into it?

Tradition and jobs

Farmers, food culture, family recipes.

Price

Who will be able to afford it?

Steps

  1. Split into two teams: For and Against.
  2. Prepare three strong arguments using the cards.
  3. Debate: opening statements, open discussion, closing statements.
  4. Vote alone and secretly. What do you really think?

Useful language

By 2040, we will have...People will be eating...The strongest argument for this is...That may be true, but...There’s no evidence that...To sum up, ...

Teacher notes. 16 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Here’s the motion for today’s debate. You’ll be in two teams, For and Against. Use the cards to prepare three strong arguments. You have four minutes.

Two teams. 4 min preparation. Then a structured debate: 1 min opening per team, 4 min open discussion, 1 min closing per team. Finish with a secret individual vote. In 1:1, the student chooses a side and you argue the other.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “By 2030 the price will fall down.” > “By 2030 the price will have fallen.”
  • “I’m not agree with this.” > “I don’t agree with this.”
  • “It’s unnatural to eat meat from laboratory.” > “It’s unnatural to eat meat from a laboratory.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

  • The new medicine was approved by the government last month.
  • Many people are sceptical about new food technology.
  • Cars and factories produce a lot of emissions.

B. Writing: a report (200-240 words)

A food company has asked you to write a short report: ‘Will customers in your country buy cultivated meat?’ Include an introduction, possible advantages, possible problems and a recommendation.

Checklist

  • Use the future continuous and the future perfect at least once each.
  • Use five words from the lesson.
  • Use headings for each section of the report.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

A report needs headings, a neutral tone and a clear recommendation. Opinions belong in the last section.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
