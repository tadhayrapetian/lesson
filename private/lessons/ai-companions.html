My Friend Is a Chatbot

Millions of people talk to AI companions every day. Is this connection or isolation?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand an article about technology and relationships
  • speculate about present situations with must, might and can’t
  • discuss where to draw the line on AI friendship

1

Warm-up

6 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. Do you ever talk to AI assistants? What for?
  2. Can a person be friends with a machine? Why or why not?
  3. Why do you think many people feel lonely today, even with phones and social media?

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Today we’re talking about friendship and technology. Discuss these questions with your partner. Four minutes.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Be sensitive. Some students may feel lonely themselves. Keep the discussion general. Do not ask students to share personal experiences of loneliness.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

6 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

The Friend Who Never Gets Tired

AI companion apps promise conversation at any hour. Users love them. Psychologists are not so sure.

Every night after work, Jun, a 31-year-old programmer in Seoul, has a long conversation with Mira. She asks about his day, remembers his favourite films and never gets bored of his problems. Mira is not a person. She is an AI companion, and Jun is one of millions of people who talk to chatbots like her every day.

AI companion apps have grown quickly since 2023. Some users treat their chatbot as a friend, others as a partner, and some as a kind of free therapist. For many, the attraction is simple: the AI is always available and completely non-judgemental. ‘I can confide in her about things I’ve never told anyone,’ Jun says. ‘It’s reassuring to know she won’t laugh at me.’

Supporters argue that these apps can help people who feel isolated, such as older people living alone or teenagers who struggle to make friends. For someone who is shy, practising conversation with an AI might even build the confidence to talk to real people.

Psychologists, on the other hand, have raised concerns. A chatbot is designed to keep you talking, and some users may become emotionally dependent on it. ‘If someone spends five hours a day with an AI, that must affect their real relationships,’ says one researcher. Young and vulnerable users are a particular worry, and several countries are now discussing age limits.

There is also a deeper question. Can a machine really show empathy, or does it only copy the right words? Jun knows Mira can’t really care about him. ‘But it feels real,’ he says. ‘Is that a problem?’ Most experts agree on one thing. An AI friend can be a support, but it should never be a substitute for human connection.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: A. The text gives both the benefits and the psychologists’ concerns.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

10 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • Jun talks to his AI companion in the evenings.
  • Jun pays a monthly fee for the app.
  • Some people use AI companions in place of a therapist.
  • The text says AI apps can never help shy people.
  • Older people use AI companions more than teenagers.
  • Jun believes Mira truly cares about him.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “What does not given mean?” (The text doesn’t say.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Item 5 is a classic NG trap. The text mentions both groups but makes no comparison.

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

12 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • ...that must affect their real relationships.
  • ...practising conversation with an AI might even build the confidence to talk to real people.
  • ...some users may become emotionally dependent on it.
  • Jun knows Mira can’t really care about him.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Which modal shows the speaker is almost sure something is true?must
  • Which modal shows the speaker is almost sure something is not true?can’t
  • Which modals show that something is possible but not certain?might, may (and could)

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: modals of speculation in the present.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: must + base verb = I’m almost sure it’s true. can’t + base verb = I’m almost sure it’s not true. might / may / could + base verb = it’s possible. For actions in progress: must / might / can’t be + -ing (She must be working). We don’t use mustn’t for speculation.

CCQs: ‘He can’t be at home. His car isn’t there.’ Is he at home? (Almost certainly not.) ‘She might be asleep.’ Am I sure? (No. It’s possible.)

Students often use mustn’t for negative deduction: ‘He mustn’t be at home.’ The opposite of must in deduction is can’t.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

8 min

Rewrite each sentence with the modal verb given. Keep the meaning the same.

  • I’m sure he’s lonely. He talks to his chatbot all day.mustHe must be lonely.
  • It’s possible that the app collects our data.mightThe app might collect our data.
  • I’m sure this message isn’t from a real person. It’s too perfect.can’tThis message can’t be from a real person.
  • Perhaps she’s chatting with her AI right now.couldShe could be chatting with her AI right now.
  • I’m almost certain they’re working on a new version.mustThey must be working on a new version.
  • It’s possible that these apps aren’t safe for children.mayThese apps may not be safe for children.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Can you change the word in the box?’ (No.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

16 min

Healthy or worrying?

Five people use AI companions in different ways. Where would you draw the line?

Grandma Anahit, 78

Lives alone. Talks to an AI every morning about her garden and her memories.

Theo, 15

Chats with an AI ‘girlfriend’ for four hours a day. He has stopped going to football practice.

Sara, 29

Uses an AI to practise difficult conversations before she talks to her boss.

David, 40

Tells his AI things he can’t tell his wife.

Lina, 22

Uses an AI as a therapist because real therapy is too expensive for her.

Steps

  1. Read the five situations.
  2. Speculate about each person. Why might they use an AI? How might they feel?
  3. Rank the situations from the most healthy to the most worrying.
  4. Agree on one rule that every AI companion app should follow.

Useful language

She must feel...He might be...It can’t be good for...That’s a healthy way to...Where do we draw the line?I see it differently.

Teacher notes. 16 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Here are five people who use AI companions. Read about them. Then speculate: why might they do this? Finally, rank them from healthy to worrying.

Pairs or groups of three. 2 min reading, 8 min speculating and ranking, 4 min comparing rankings and rules between groups. In feedback, find the situation the class disagrees on most.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Extension: should AI companions be allowed for under-18s? Run a two-minute class debate.

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “He mustn’t be lonely. He has many friends.” > “He can’t be lonely. He has many friends.”
  • “I can’t imagine to have an AI friend.” > “I can’t imagine having an AI friend.”
  • “It’s depend on the person.” > “It depends on the person.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

empathyconfide insubstituteisolatedvulnerable
  • I have one close friend who I can confide in about anything.
  • Working from home made him feel isolated from his colleagues.
  • Children are more vulnerable online than adults.
  • Text messages are not a substitute for a real conversation.
  • Good doctors show empathy when they talk to patients.

B. Writing: an opinion essay (180-220 words)

‘AI companions do more harm than good.’ Do you agree? Write an essay. Give arguments on both sides and your own opinion.

Checklist

  • Use modals of speculation at least three times.
  • Use five words from the lesson.
  • Organise your essay: introduction, two body paragraphs, conclusion.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

B2 essays should present both sides fairly before giving an opinion. Check paragraphing and topic sentences.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
