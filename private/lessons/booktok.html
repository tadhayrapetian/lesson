Warm-up

5 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. What was the last book you read? Did you like it?
  2. Where do you get ideas for books, films or series?
  3. Do you prefer paper books, e-books or audiobooks? Why?

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Let’s talk about reading. Discuss the questions with your partner. You have three minutes.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Students who don’t read books can talk about films or series. Accept all answers.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

5 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

How TikTok Got Teenagers Reading

A few years ago, people said young people didn’t read. Then BookTok happened.

A few years ago, many people said young people didn’t read anymore. Then something surprising happened. On TikTok, readers started posting short videos about books that made them cry, laugh or scream. People called this world ‘BookTok’, and it changed the book industry.

BookTok videos are usually very emotional. A reader holds up a book, says ‘This book destroyed me’, and millions of people want to know why. Users don’t write long reviews. They share feelings. They talk about characters who they love and endings that shocked them.

The results have been huge. Books that came out ten years ago suddenly became bestsellers. Many bookshops now have a table with a sign that says ‘As seen on BookTok’. Publishers look for writers who are popular on the app, and some authors who started online now have big book deals.

BookTok has also created a community. Young readers find friends who like the same genre, from fantasy to romance. ‘In my school, nobody reads,’ says Ellie, 16. ‘But online I’ve found people who understand me.’ Some even meet in real life at book clubs in local cafés.

Not everyone is happy. Some critics say BookTok only recommends a small number of books, so the same titles are everywhere. Others say people choose books for the cover or a shocking plot twist, not the writing. But many teachers disagree. ‘Any book that a teenager chooses to read is a good book,’ says one librarian.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: C. The text describes the effect of BookTok and ends with the critics’ view.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

Close read

8 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T) or false (F)?

  • BookTok videos are usually long, careful reviews.
  • Some older books became popular again because of BookTok.
  • Many bookshops have tables with BookTok books.
  • Ellie has many friends at school who read.
  • Critics say BookTok recommends too many different books.
  • The librarian thinks any book a teenager chooses is good.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T) or false (F)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “Do you need to show where the answer is?” (Yes.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

10 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • They talk about characters who they love.
  • ...readers started posting short videos about books that made them cry, laugh or scream.
  • Publishers look for writers who are popular on the app.
  • Any book that a teenager chooses to read is a good book.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Which word do we use for people?who (or that)
  • Which words do we use for things?that or which
  • In ‘characters who they love’, can we remove who? Why?Yes. who is the object (they love the characters). We can leave out the pronoun when a new subject follows.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: defining relative clauses.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: Defining relative clauses give essential information about a noun. who / that for people. which / that for things. where for places. We can leave out who / which / that when it is the object of the clause: the book (that) I read.

CCQs: ‘The girl who recommended this book is my sister.’ Do we know which girl? (Yes, the one who recommended the book.)

Common mistake: ‘The book which I read it was great.’ Don’t repeat the object pronoun.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

Practice

7 min

Join the two sentences with the word given.

  • I read a book. It made me cry.thatI read a book that made me cry.
  • This is the author. She wrote my favourite novel.whoThis is the author who wrote my favourite novel.
  • That’s the café. We have our book club there.whereThat’s the café where we have our book club.
  • I have a friend. He reads 50 books a year.whoI have a friend who reads 50 books a year.
  • Fantasy is a genre. Many teenagers love it.thatFantasy is a genre that many teenagers love.
  • We went to a bookshop. It has a BookTok table.whichWe went to a bookshop which has a BookTok table.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Do you write two sentences or one?’ (One.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

12 min

The 60-second pitch

Recommend a book, film or series that you love, BookTok style. You have 60 seconds. Make your partner want it.

The genre

What kind of story is it?

The hook

One sentence that makes people curious.

A character

Someone who you love or hate.

The feeling

How did it make you feel, and why?

Steps

  1. Think of a book, film or series that made you feel something strong.
  2. Make notes for the four cards. Notes only, not full sentences.
  3. Give your pitch. Your partner asks two questions.
  4. Choose one recommendation you really want to try.

Useful language

It’s a story about a girl who...It’s set in a place where...The best part is...It made me cry. / It made me laugh.I’d recommend it to people who...No spoilers, but...

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: You’re going to recommend something you love, BookTok style. You have three minutes to make notes. Then you have 60 seconds to sell it.

Individual preparation, 3 min. Pairs or groups of three, 60 seconds each plus two questions. Finish with a class vote for the best pitch.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Extension: students record their pitch on their phones as a homework video.

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

3 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 3 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “This is the book who I told you about.” > “This is the book that I told you about.”
  • “The man which wrote it is French.” > “The man who wrote it is French.”
  • “I recommend you this book.” > “I recommend this book to you.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

reviewsrecommendplotbestsellergenre
  • My favourite genre is science fiction.
  • The film had a great plot, but the actors were weak.
  • Can you recommend a good podcast for learning English?
  • Before I buy a product, I always read the reviews online.
  • The novel became a bestseller and sold two million copies.

B. Writing: a recommendation post (110-140 words)

Write a post for a reading website about a book, film or series you love. Describe the genre and the plot without spoilers, and explain who you would recommend it to.

Checklist

  • Use at least three relative clauses with who, which, that or where.
  • Use four words from the lesson.
  • Finish by saying who should read or watch it.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

Check that students don’t repeat the pronoun inside the relative clause.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
