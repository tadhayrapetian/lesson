Seeing Is No Longer Believing

Synthetic media can put words in anyone’s mouth. What happens to trust?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand a complex article about AI, fraud and trust
  • make deductions about the past with must have, can’t have and might have
  • evaluate evidence and justify editorial decisions

3

First read

7 min

Read the text quickly. Which statement best expresses the writer’s main argument?

The Call That Cost $25 Million

Deepfakes have moved from internet jokes to boardroom fraud and election campaigns. The real danger may not be the fakes themselves.

In early 2024, a finance employee at the Hong Kong office of a multinational company joined a video call with his chief financial officer and several colleagues. He had been suspicious of an earlier email, but on screen everything looked and sounded right. Following instructions, he transferred around 25 million US dollars to a series of bank accounts. Every other person on that call, it later emerged, had been a deepfake. The fraudsters must have studied hours of publicly available footage to impersonate the executives so convincingly.

Cases like this show how quickly synthetic media has evolved. A few years ago, deepfakes were glitchy curiosities: faces that flickered, voices that sounded robotic. Today, a few seconds of someone’s voice can be enough to clone it, and generative tools can produce plausible video in minutes. Criminals have used cloned voices to call parents, pretending to be a child in trouble, and fake audio of politicians has circulated before elections in several countries.

Yet some researchers argue that the most serious threat is not deception but disbelief. As people learn that any recording might be fabricated, they may stop trusting genuine evidence too. Legal scholars call this the ‘liar’s dividend’: a politician caught on camera can simply claim the footage was faked, and enough people will accept it. In this sense, deepfakes don’t just spread falsehoods. They sow doubt about everything, gradually eroding the shared reality that democratic debate depends on.

Technology companies are fighting back. Some now attach digital watermarks or signed ‘content credentials’ to images produced by their tools, so that platforms and journalists can authenticate where a file came from. Detection software is improving too, although it is locked in an arms race with the generators, and no system is foolproof.

Ultimately, experts say, the best defence may be old-fashioned: slowing down. Before sharing a shocking clip, scrutinise the source, look for confirmation from reliable outlets, and be wary of anything designed to provoke outrage. In the Hong Kong case, a single phone call to a known number might have prevented the loss. The employee can’t have imagined that every face on the screen was fake. Next time, few of us will be able to afford that assumption.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 4 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: C. Paragraph 3 is the turning point: ‘not deception but disbelief’.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

Close read

12 min

Read again. Answer the questions in your own words.

  • Why did the employee eventually transfer the money?The video call looked and sounded genuine, apparently with the CFO and colleagues, so his earlier suspicion disappeared.
  • How has deepfake technology changed, according to paragraph 2?It has moved from obvious, glitchy fakes to realistic voice clones and video that can be made quickly from small samples.
  • Explain the ‘liar’s dividend’ in your own words.When fakes are common, people caught on real recordings can deny them by saying they are fake, and some people will believe them.
  • Why does the writer describe detection as an ‘arms race’?Detection tools and generation tools keep improving in response to each other, so neither side wins for good.
  • What simple step might have prevented the Hong Kong loss?Calling the executives back on a known phone number to check the request.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Answer the questions in your own words. Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “Can you copy whole sentences from the text?” (No, use your own words.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Push for paraphrase. At C1, answers copied from the text don’t show understanding.

Say: Good. Now let’s look at how the writer talks about things that probably happened.

5

Language focus

14 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • The fraudsters must have studied hours of publicly available footage.
  • In the Hong Kong case, a single phone call to a known number might have prevented the loss.
  • The employee can’t have imagined that every face on the screen was fake.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Which modal shows the writer is almost certain about a past action?must have studied
  • can’t have imagined is the opposite of which modal? What does it show?must have. Near certainty that something didn’t happen or wasn’t true.
  • Is might have prevented the same as must have prevented? What does it mean here?No. might have = a possibility. Here it is unrealised: the call wasn’t made, so it is hypothetical.

Teacher notes. 14 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: past modals of deduction.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: must have + past participle = almost certain it happened. can’t / couldn’t have + past participle = almost certain it didn’t happen. might / may / could have + past participle = it possibly happened, or it was possible but didn’t happen. Passive: must have been + past participle (It must have been faked). Continuous: might have been + -ing.

CCQs: ‘She might have seen the email.’ Do we know if she saw it? (No.) ‘He can’t have known.’ How sure am I that he didn’t know? (Almost certain.)

Common mistakes: ‘He mustn’t have known’ (use can’t have) and ‘must had’ (use have + past participle). Drill the weak forms must’ve, can’t’ve, might’ve.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

10 min

Rewrite each sentence with the modal given. Keep the meaning as close as possible.

  • I’m sure the video was edited.mustThe video must have been edited.
  • It’s impossible that she recorded that. She was abroad.can’tShe can’t have recorded that. She was abroad.
  • Perhaps the criminals used an old recording.mightThe criminals might have used an old recording.
  • I’m certain they weren’t expecting such a convincing fake.couldn’tThey couldn’t have been expecting such a convincing fake.
  • It’s possible that the email was sent by a bot.mayThe email may have been sent by a bot.
  • I’m sure the employee felt terrible afterwards.mustThe employee must have felt terrible afterwards.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Are these sentences about the present or the past?’ (The past.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

20 min

The newsroom

You are editors at a news website. Four viral clips have just arrived. Decide what to do with each one.

Clip A

A 30-second video of a famous footballer insulting fans. Posted by an anonymous account. Two million views in an hour.

Clip B

Audio of a mayor apparently offering a bribe. Sent to your newsroom by email. The mayor says it’s fake.

Clip C

A photo of a flooded city centre after a storm. Shared by a local resident. Other photos look similar.

Clip D

A video of a CEO announcing that the company is bankrupt. Its share price has already fallen 20%.

Steps

  1. For each clip, speculate. What must, might or can’t have happened?
  2. Decide what checks you would do before publishing.
  3. Decide: publish, publish with a warning, or don’t publish.
  4. Discuss: who should be responsible for stopping deepfakes: platforms, governments or users?

Useful language

It must have been...Someone could have...That can’t have been...Before we publish, we’d need to...The burden of proof lies with...I’m inclined to think...

Teacher notes. 20 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: You’re the editorial team of a news website. Four clips have just arrived. Read them. For each one, speculate about what happened and decide what to do.

Groups of three or four as a newsroom team. 3 min reading, 10 min discussion, 5 min reporting decisions and the final question. Encourage hedging and past deduction.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Extension: is the liar’s dividend more dangerous than the fakes themselves? Quick class vote with one-sentence justifications.

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “It mustn’t have been real.” > “It can’t have been real.”
  • “He must have saw the video.” > “He must have seen the video.”
  • “This information is not trustable.” > “This information isn’t reliable.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.
