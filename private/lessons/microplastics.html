Plastic Inside Us

Tiny pieces of plastic have been found in our blood, our lungs and even our brains. What do we really know?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • complete a gapped text by following the logic of a text
  • understand detail, reference words and the writer’s view
  • discuss personal and global solutions to an environmental problem

Background: Microplastics have been found in human blood, lungs, placentas and, in a 2025 study, brain tissue. A 2024 study linked microplastics in arterial plaque to higher risk of heart attack and stroke. Talks on a global plastics treaty have struggled to reach agreement.

First read

6 min

Plastic Inside Us

1Plastic is everywhere: in our clothes, our phones, our food packaging. Over time, it breaks into smaller and smaller pieces. Pieces smaller than five millimetres are called microplastics, and the smallest, nanoplastics, are invisible to the human eye. (1) ___

2For years, the main concern was wildlife. Sea birds, fish and turtles swallow plastic, which can block their stomachs. But in the last few years, the focus has shifted to humans. Researchers have found microplastics in human blood, lungs and placentas. (2) ___

3How does plastic get into our bodies? Mostly through what we eat, drink and breathe. Bottled water, sea salt and seafood all contain particles, and heating food in plastic containers can release more. (3) ___ Every time we wash synthetic clothes, thousands of fibres go down the drain.

4The big question is whether this is dangerous. (4) ___ Some laboratory studies suggest microplastics can cause inflammation, and one study found that heart patients with plastic in their arteries had a higher risk of heart attack and stroke. But scientists stress that these studies show a link, not proof that plastic causes the damage.

5While research continues, experts suggest some simple steps. Drink tap water instead of bottled water where it is safe. Don’t heat food in plastic. Choose natural fabrics when you can. (5) ___

6Ultimately, many scientists argue, the solution is not individual but global: producing less plastic in the first place. Countries have been negotiating an international plastics treaty, but they have struggled to agree.

Read quickly. What is the main purpose of the article?

  • The writer wants to...

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual.

Say: Read quickly. Ignore the gaps for now. What is the main purpose? You have three minutes.

Check the board predictions from the warm-up.

4

Missing sentences

14 min

Read again. Choose the sentence a-e that fits each gap 1-5.

  • Gap 1c
  • Gap 2e
  • Gap 3d
  • Gap 4a
  • Gap 5b
  • aThe honest answer is that nobody knows for sure yet.
  • bNone of these will remove plastic from your life completely, but they can reduce your exposure.
  • cScientists now believe they are almost everywhere on the planet, from the deepest oceans to the top of Mount Everest.
  • dEven the air inside our homes is full of tiny fibres from carpets and clothes.
  • eIn 2025, a study even reported plastic particles in samples of human brain tissue.

Teacher notes. 14 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Now find the missing sentences. Read the text before and after each gap carefully.

Teach the strategy: look for links. In the ‘almost everywhere’ sentence, they needs a plural noun before it (microplastics, nanoplastics). The ‘tiny fibres’ sentence connects to the next sentence about washing clothes. In the ‘None of these’ sentence, these refers to the steps before the gap.

Pairs justify each answer with a linking word. This is a common B2 exam task (FCE Reading Part 6).

5

Close read

10 min

Choose the correct answer.

  • According to paragraph 2, what has changed in recent years?
  • Which of these is NOT mentioned as a source of microplastics?
  • What do the heart studies show?
  • What is the writer’s view of individual actions?
  • Why does the writer mention the plastics treaty?

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Choose the correct answer. Underline the evidence in the text.

Focus on item 3: link vs cause. This is an important critical reading skill.

6

Reference and meaning

8 min

What do these words refer to? Then find the words or phrases.

  • which in ‘which can block their stomachs’ (paragraph 2)the plastic that birds, fish and turtles swallow
  • these studies (paragraph 4)the laboratory studies and the study of heart patients
  • these in ‘None of these’ (gap 5)the simple steps: tap water, not heating food in plastic, natural fabrics
  • a verb in paragraph 3 that means ‘let out’ or ‘set free’release
  • a phrase in paragraph 6 that means ‘at the start, before anything else’in the first place

Teacher notes. 8 min. Pairs.

Say: Writers use words like which and these to connect ideas. What do they refer to?

Reference words are key for gapped texts. Link this back to stage 4.

7

Speaking: what would work?

17 min

Which solutions would make the biggest difference? Which are realistic?

Ban it

Ban single-use plastic bottles and bags.

Tax it

Put a tax on all plastic packaging.

Label it

Clothes must show how much plastic is in the fabric.

Change habits

Tap water, natural fabrics, no plastic in the microwave.

Wait and research

Fund more research before making new laws.

Steps

  1. Rank the solutions from most to least effective.
  2. Now rank them from most to least realistic in your country.
  3. Compare the two lists. What surprises you?
  4. Agree on one action for governments and one for individuals.

Useful language

The evidence suggests...That would only work if...Realistically, ...In the first place, we should...I’m not convinced it would make a difference.It’s a step in the right direction.

Teacher notes. 17 min. Groups of three or four.

Say: Look at the five solutions. First rank them by effect. Then by how realistic they are in your country.

Groups. 3 min reading, 10 min ranking and discussing, 4 min reporting.

One-to-one: the student ranks; you challenge with ‘But what about the cost?’ and ‘Who pays?’

8

Reflection and feedback

4 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 4 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘The plastic is everywhere.’ > ‘Plastic is everywhere.’
  • ‘It can make an inflammation.’ > ‘It can cause inflammation.’
  • ‘microplastics are founded in blood’ > ‘microplastics have been found in blood’

Homework

Set at the end

A. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

swallowtreatyparticlesexposuresynthetic

  • Polyester is a synthetic material.
  • Too much sun exposure can damage your skin.
  • Be careful not to swallow the cherry stone.
  • The countries signed a treaty to protect the ocean.

Write an opinion essay (150-180 words).

‘Governments should ban single-use plastics completely.’ Do you agree? Give reasons and examples, and consider one argument against your view.

Checklist

  • Use at least five words from the lesson.
  • Use reference words (this, these, which) to connect ideas.
  • Write an introduction, two body paragraphs and a conclusion.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, complete Part A and write the essay.

Mark cohesion: do students use reference words and linkers well?
