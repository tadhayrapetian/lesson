Facial recognition is spreading through streets, shops and stadiums. Learn the precise language of privacy, power and rights.

Background: Police in some countries use live facial recognition in public places. The EU’s AI Act (2024) strictly limits real-time remote biometric identification by police in public spaces. A 2019 US government study (NIST) found higher false positive rates for some demographic groups in many algorithms.

2

Read

12 min

The End of Anonymity?

Walk through a busy station in London and you may pass a white van with cameras on its roof. Inside, software compares the faces of passers-by with a watchlist of wanted people. If there’s a match, officers are alerted within seconds. Supporters say live facial recognition helps police find dangerous suspects quickly. Critics call it the end of anonymity in public space.

The technology is being deployed far beyond policing. Some shops use it to identify suspected shoplifters, stadiums to keep out banned fans, and airports to speed up boarding. In some countries, cameras track citizens’ movements on a scale that would have been unimaginable a generation ago.

Civil liberties groups argue that mass surveillance infringes on the right to privacy and has a chilling effect: people may avoid protests or certain neighbourhoods if they know they are being watched. There are also concerns about accuracy. Studies have found that some systems produce more false positives for certain groups, raising the risk that innocent people are wrongly stopped. And unlike a password, a face cannot be changed if the data is stolen.

Regulators have responded in different ways. The European Union’s AI Act places strict limits on real-time facial recognition by police in public spaces, allowing it only in narrow circumstances, such as searching for victims of serious crimes. Some cities have imposed a blanket ban on its use by public authorities. Elsewhere, there are few rules at all.

Defenders insist that the technology is a tool, not a threat, and that proper safeguards, such as independent oversight and deleting data quickly, can protect rights. The question for societies is not only whether the technology works, but who controls it, and whether citizens have meaningfully consented to living under its gaze.

Answer the questions in your own words.

  • How does live facial recognition work, according to paragraph 1?Cameras film people, software compares their faces with a watchlist, and police are alerted if there’s a match.
  • Why might people avoid protests, according to critics?Because they know they’re being watched. This is the chilling effect.
  • Why is a stolen face more serious than a stolen password?You can change a password, but you can’t change your face.
  • What does the writer say the key question is?Not only whether the technology works, but who controls it and whether people have really agreed to it.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the article. Answer the questions in your own words.

Students highlight any word combination that seems new or ‘expert’. We’ll study them next.

5

Word families

12 min

Complete each sentence with the correct form of the word in capitals.

  • Critics question the accuracy of the software. (ACCURATE)
  • Collecting data without permission is legally questionable. (QUESTION)
  • Citizens have a right to remain anonymous in public. (ANONYMITY)
  • The system incorrectly identified him as a suspect. (CORRECT)
  • Strict rules govern the storage of personal data. (STORE)
  • Many fear the technology will be misused by future governments. (USE)
  • The police must justify every use of the system. (JUSTICE)
  • The scheme raised serious ethical concerns. (ETHICS)

Teacher notes. 12 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Change the word in capitals. You may need a prefix, a suffix or both.

Board the prefixes: mis- (wrongly), in- / un- (not). Many C1 exam word-formation answers need two changes, like incorrectly.

Feedback: ask students what part of speech each gap needs before they give the answer.

7

Speaking: the public hearing

21 min

Your city council is holding a public hearing: Should the police use live facial recognition in the city centre?

Police chief

You want to use it to find wanted suspects and missing people quickly.

Civil liberties lawyer

You believe it infringes on privacy and creates a chilling effect.

Shop owners’ association

Shoplifting is rising. You want to use the technology in shops too.

Technology company

You sell the system. You say accuracy is improving and safeguards are possible.

Resident

Last year, a camera system wrongly identified you, and you were stopped in front of your children.

Steps

  1. Read your role. Prepare your position, using at least five expressions from the lesson.
  2. Each speaker gives a formal two-minute statement.
  3. Open discussion. Ask each other difficult questions.
  4. Vote: ban, allow with strict safeguards, or allow. Write a three-sentence decision.

Useful language

With respect, ...This would inevitably infringe on...The risk of false positives is...Provided that there is independent oversight, ...I’m not persuaded that...In narrow circumstances, such as...

Teacher notes. 21 min. Groups of four or five.

Say: The council is holding a public hearing. Read your role and prepare a formal statement. You have five minutes.

Groups of four or five. 5 min preparation, 10 min statements and discussion, 6 min voting and reporting.

This is a chance to practise formal register. Reward precise vocabulary in feedback.

One-to-one: the student chooses two roles and argues both; you chair and ask hard questions.

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘make a blanket ban’ > ‘impose a blanket ban’
  • ‘civil freedoms’ > ‘civil liberties’
  • ‘The cameras follow people’s moves.’ > ‘The cameras track people’s movements.’

Homework

Set at the end

A. Each sentence has one wrong word or collocation. Write the correct sentence.

  • The police made a blanket forbid on protests.The police imposed a blanket ban on protests.
  • The system made many false positives results.The system produced many false positives.
  • The cameras follow people’s moves.The cameras track people’s movements.
  • We need an independent overseeing.We need independent oversight.
  • Civil freedoms are under threat.Civil liberties are under threat.

Write a report (250-280 words).

Write a report for your city council: ‘Should our city use facial recognition in public places?’ Include an introduction, the benefits, the risks and a recommendation with safeguards.

Checklist

  • Use at least eight words or collocations from the lesson.
  • Use a formal register and headings.
  • Make your recommendation clear and specific.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, correct the sentences and write the report.

Mark the report for register, range of vocabulary and clarity of recommendation.
