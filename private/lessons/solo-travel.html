Table for One, Please

Travelling alone is more popular than ever. Is it brave, lonely or the best thing you can do?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand an article about travelling alone
  • give advice and talk about rules with must, have to and should
  • plan a trip and make recommendations

Lesson 04

Teacher's overview

Length: 60 minutes plus homework.

Main aim: Speaking: to give practical advice and plan a trip together.

Subsidiary aims: Reading for gist and detail. Travel vocabulary.

Language aim: Modals of obligation, prohibition and advice.

Background: Solo travel has grown strongly since the pandemic. Travel companies report many more bookings for one person, especially from women and older travellers.

1

Warm-up

5 min
The world is a book, and those who do not travel read only one page.A saying often attributed to Saint Augustine

Talk with a partner.

  1. Do you agree with the saying? Why or why not?
  2. Have you ever travelled alone? Where?
  3. Would you prefer to travel alone, with friends or with family? Why?

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Read the saying. Do you agree with it? Then discuss the questions with your partner.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

5 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

Table for One, Please

More and more people are packing a bag and travelling alone. Here’s what they say.

Ten years ago, many people thought solo travel was strange or even sad. Now it is one of the biggest trends in tourism. Travel companies say more and more people book trips for one. Many of them are women, and many are over 50.

Why go alone? ‘You don’t have to agree with anyone,’ says Nadia, 34, who has visited 20 countries by herself. ‘You wake up and decide. If you want to stay in a museum all day, you can.’ Many solo travellers say the biggest gift of the trip is confidence. When you get lost in a new city and find your way, you feel you can do anything.

It is also easier to meet people. ‘In a hostel kitchen, everyone talks to everyone,’ says Max, 22. ‘And when you are alone, locals often start a conversation with you.’

Of course, there are challenges. Eating alone in a restaurant can feel strange at first. Single rooms are often expensive, so you should plan your budget carefully. Safety is important too. ‘You must tell someone at home where you are,’ says Nadia. ‘And you shouldn’t share your plans with strangers.’

Experts have simple advice for first-timers. Start with a short trip to a country that feels safe and easy. You don’t have to plan every minute, but a basic itinerary helps. ‘The first day is the hardest,’ says Max. ‘After that, you leave your comfort zone and you don’t want to go back.’

Teacher notes. 5 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: C. The text covers the trend, the benefits, the challenges and advice.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

8 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T) or false (F)?

  • Solo travel seemed strange to many people ten years ago.
  • Nadia has travelled to 20 countries with friends.
  • Max thinks it is hard to meet people in hostels.
  • Single rooms often cost a lot.
  • Nadia says you can share your plans with strangers.
  • Experts suggest a short first trip.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T) or false (F)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “Do you need to show where the answer is?” (Yes.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

10 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • You don’t have to agree with anyone.
  • ...so you should plan your budget carefully.
  • You must tell someone at home where you are.
  • And you shouldn’t share your plans with strangers.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Which modal means ‘it’s a good idea’?should
  • Which modal means ‘it’s very important, it’s necessary’?must
  • Does don’t have to mean ‘it’s not allowed’ or ‘it’s not necessary’?It’s not necessary. You can choose.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: must, have to, don’t have to, should.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: must / have to = necessary. don’t have to = not necessary, you can choose. mustn’t = not allowed. should / shouldn’t = advice. Modals + base verb, without to. have to changes: he has to, they had to.

CCQs: ‘You don’t have to book a hotel.’ Is it a problem if you book one? (No.) ‘You mustn’t take photos here.’ Can I take photos? (No.)

Students often confuse mustn’t and don’t have to. Draw two columns on the board: not allowed and not necessary.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

Practice

7 min

Complete the travel tips with the words in the box. Use each one once.

have toshoulddon’t have tomustshouldn’tmustn’t
  • You must have a valid passport to fly to another country.
  • Hostels are cheap, but you don’t have to stay in them. Hotels are fine too.
  • You mustn’t leave your bag alone at the airport. It’s not allowed.
  • I think you should learn a few words of the local language.
  • In some countries, you have to get a visa before you travel.
  • You shouldn’t walk alone in dark streets late at night.

Teacher notes. 7 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘How many times can you use each word?’ (Once.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

12 min

Plan a first solo trip

Three people want to travel alone for the first time. Choose one and plan their trip.

Anna, 19

First trip abroad. Small budget. Loves music and meeting new people.

Mr Petrosyan, 62

Just retired. Wants culture and good food. A bit worried about safety.

Leila, 28

Very busy job. Wants to relax for five days. Doesn’t like crowds.

Steps

  1. Choose one traveller.
  2. Choose a destination and plan a short itinerary together.
  3. Give five pieces of advice with must, should and don’t have to.
  4. Present your plan. Other students ask one question each.

Useful language

She should definitely...He doesn’t have to...It’s important to...What about...?Great idea, and...I’m not sure that’s safe for...

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Three people want to travel alone for the first time. Choose one. Plan a short trip for them and give them advice. You have two minutes to choose and think.

Pairs choose different travellers if possible. 2 min preparation, 7 min planning, 3 min presentations.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

3 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 3 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “You must to book a hostel.” > “You must book a hostel.”
  • “You don’t must go there.” > “You don’t have to go there.”
  • “I want to travel solo in next summer.” > “I want to travel solo next summer.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

9

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

confidencebudgethostelcomfort zonelocal
  • We stayed in a hostel with eight beds in one room. It was cheap and fun.
  • Speaking in public helped me build my confidence.
  • My budget for the trip is 500 euros, including food.
  • Ask a local for the best place to eat. They always know.
  • Trying a new sport took me out of my comfort zone.

B. Writing: an email of advice (110-140 words)

Your friend wants to travel alone for the first time. Write an email. Suggest a place, give at least four pieces of advice and say why solo travel is a good idea.

Checklist

  • Use should, must and don’t have to.
  • Use four words from the lesson.
  • Use a friendly opening and ending.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

Look for a clear difference between must and should in the student’s advice.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
