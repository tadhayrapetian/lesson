Holding On Tight

From quiet quitting to job hugging: what workers want in an uncertain economy

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand an article about workplace trends
  • report what people say with verbs like admit, warn and advise
  • give balanced advice about career decisions

Warm-up

6 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. What makes a job a good job: money, people, security or something else?
  2. Would you prefer a safe, boring job or an exciting, risky one? Why?
  3. How often do people change jobs in your country?

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Let’s talk about work. Discuss the questions with your partner. Four minutes.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

6 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

Why Nobody Is Quitting Anymore

A few years ago, workers were leaving their jobs in record numbers. Now they’re doing the opposite.

In 2021, the world talked about the ‘Great Resignation’. Millions of workers left their jobs in search of better pay, more freedom or simply a change. Then came ‘quiet quitting’: people who kept their jobs but did only the minimum. Now there’s a new phrase, ‘job hugging’. Workers are holding on to their jobs as tightly as they can.

The reason is uncertainty. After a wave of layoffs in tech and other industries, and with AI changing many roles, the job market feels less safe. ‘Two years ago I would have changed jobs without thinking,’ admits Priya, a marketing specialist in London. ‘Now I value job security more than anything.’ Surveys suggest that fewer people are planning to leave their jobs than a few years ago.

For employers, this sounds like good news. But some experts warn that job hugging has a dark side. Workers who stay put only because they are afraid often become disengaged. They don’t complain, but they don’t bring energy or new ideas either. ‘Staying in the wrong job for too long can make you stagnate,’ one career coach explained.

There’s also the question of money. In the past, the fastest way to get a pay rise was often to change companies. Job huggers may stay on the same salary for years, waiting for a promotion that never comes. Some career experts advise workers to keep learning new skills, so they are ready to move up the career ladder when the market improves.

Is job hugging a smart strategy or a trap? It depends on why you’re staying. ‘If you like your job, staying is great,’ Priya says. ‘But I’ve promised myself one thing. When the time comes to leave, I’ll do it properly. I won’t burn bridges.’

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: C. The text explains the trend, its causes and its risks.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

10 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • Quiet quitters left their jobs to find better pay.
  • Two years ago, Priya would have changed jobs more easily.
  • Priya has worked at her current company for ten years.
  • Experts say job huggers are always happy and productive.
  • Changing companies was often a quick way to earn more.
  • Priya’s manager has offered her a promotion.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “What does not given mean?” (The text doesn’t say.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

12 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • ‘Two years ago I would have changed jobs without thinking,’ admits Priya.
  • But some experts warn that job hugging has a dark side.
  • Surveys suggest that fewer people are planning to leave.
  • Some career experts advise workers to keep learning new skills.
  • I’ve promised myself one thing.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Which verbs are followed by that + clause?admit, warn, suggest (promise can also take that)
  • Which verb is followed by object + to + infinitive?advise: advise workers to keep. Also tell, warn, encourage, remind.
  • Why do writers use admit or warn instead of say?They show the speaker’s attitude or purpose. admit = say something you aren’t proud of. warn = tell someone about a danger.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: reporting verbs.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: verb + that clause: admit, claim, explain, suggest, warn. verb + object + to-infinitive: advise, warn, encourage, persuade, remind. verb + to-infinitive: promise, refuse, agree, offer. verb + -ing: deny, recommend, suggest. After a past reporting verb, backshift tenses when needed.

CCQs: ‘He refused to work overtime.’ Did he work overtime? (No.) Did someone ask him to? (Yes.)

Common mistake: ‘She suggested me to apply.’ Correct: ‘She suggested that I apply’ or ‘She suggested applying.’

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

8 min

Report the sentences with the verb given.

  • ‘I’ll send you the report tomorrow,’ said Ana.promisedAna promised to send me the report the next day.
  • ‘You should update your CV,’ my manager said to me.advisedMy manager advised me to update my CV.
  • ‘I didn’t take the money!’ said Tom.deniedTom denied taking the money.
  • ‘OK, I was wrong about the deadline,’ said the boss.admittedThe boss admitted (that) he / she had been wrong about the deadline.
  • ‘No, I won’t work at the weekend,’ said Lena.refusedLena refused to work at the weekend.
  • ‘Don’t sign the contract before you read it,’ the lawyer told us.warnedThe lawyer warned us not to sign the contract before we read it.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Do you need to change the tense?’ (Sometimes. Think about it.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

16 min

Stay or go?

Four people can’t decide whether to keep their jobs. Give them advice.

Mark, 34

Safe job at a bank for eight years. Bored. A start-up offers him 20% more money but no guarantees.

Elen, 26

Loves her team. No promotion in three years. Her manager keeps saying ‘next year’.

Rafael, 45

Two children. His company has announced layoffs. He might lose his job, but he isn’t sure.

Jess, 29

Long hours, good salary. Feels burnt out. Dreams of becoming a teacher.

Steps

  1. Read the four cases. Choose two.
  2. Discuss the risks of staying and the risks of leaving for each person.
  3. Decide what you would advise each person to do.
  4. Report your advice to another pair: We advised Mark to... We warned Elen that...

Useful language

The main risk of staying is...On the other hand, if she leaves...In his position, I’d...We’d advise him to...We warned her that...It’s a gamble, but...

Teacher notes. 16 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Four people can’t decide whether to stay in their jobs. Choose two of them. Discuss the risks and decide on your advice. Then you’ll report it to another pair.

Pairs. 3 min reading and choosing, 8 min discussion, 5 min reporting to another pair. Listen for the patterns of the reporting verbs.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

In 1:1, the student chooses two cases and you play the person. The student advises you directly, then reports the advice back.

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “My boss suggested me to take a course.” > “My boss suggested that I take a course.”
  • “He explained me the problem.” > “He explained the problem to me.”
  • “I’m working in this company since 2020.” > “I’ve been working at this company since 2020.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

B. Writing: an email of advice (180-220 words)

A friend has a safe but boring job and an offer from an exciting new company. Write a reply to their email. Discuss the risks of staying and leaving, report advice from people you know, and give your recommendation.

Checklist

  • Use at least four reporting verbs with the correct pattern.
  • Use five words from the lesson.
  • Balance both options before your recommendation.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

Check the patterns after suggest and advise. These are the most frequent errors.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
