Born Online

Parents share their children’s lives on social media. Who gets to decide?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • understand an article about children’s privacy online
  • talk about past regrets with the third conditional and should have
  • agree on rules for sharing children’s lives online

1

Warm-up

6 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. Do your family or friends post photos of their children online?
  2. Are there any photos of you as a child online? How do you feel about that?
  3. At what age should children decide what is posted about them?

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Let’s talk about photos and privacy. Discuss the questions with your partner.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Some students are parents. Keep the tone non-judgemental. The aim is discussion, not blame.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

6 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

The First Generation Born Online

Today’s teenagers had a digital footprint before they could walk. Now some of them are asking: why did nobody ask me?

When Clara turned 16, she searched her own name online. She found hundreds of photos: her first bath, her first day at school, a video of her crying after a bad haircut. Her mother had posted them all on a public account. ‘If she had asked me, I would have said no to half of them,’ Clara says. ‘Some of them are really embarrassing.’

Clara is part of the first generation whose childhood was shared online almost from birth. The habit even has a name: sharenting. Most parents who do it are well-meaning. They want to share proud moments with family and friends. But babies can’t give consent, and by the time children are old enough to understand, their digital footprint is already huge.

Experts point to several risks. Photos with school uniforms or location data can show strangers where a child lives. Names, birthdays and pet names, the classic answers to security questions, can be used for identity theft years later. And once a photo has been shared, it is almost impossible to delete completely, even if parents change their privacy settings.

The problem is bigger when parents monetise their children’s lives. ‘Family influencers’ can earn thousands of dollars from a single sponsored video. Critics say some of these families exploit their children, filming them every day like actors, but without a salary or a choice. Several former child stars of family channels have spoken publicly about how it affected them.

Governments are starting to act. In 2024, France passed legislation that protects children’s rights over their own image, and some US states now require that money earned by child influencers is saved for them. Clara’s mother has since deleted most of the photos. ‘If I had known about the risks, I would never have posted them,’ she says. ‘I should have thought more carefully. I just wanted to show how much I loved her.’

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: C. The text is balanced: parents mean well, but there are risks, and laws are changing.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

10 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • Clara was happy with all the photos she found.
  • Clara’s friends had seen the embarrassing photos.
  • Most parents who share photos want to harm their children.
  • Information like pet names can be useful to criminals.
  • Clara’s mother earned money from the photos.
  • France has a law about children’s rights over their image.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “What does not given mean?” (The text doesn’t say.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

12 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • If she had asked me, I would have said no to half of them.
  • If I had known about the risks, I would never have posted them.
  • I should have thought more carefully.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Did Clara’s mother ask her? Did Clara say no?No and no. The sentence imagines a different past.
  • What forms are in each part of the conditional sentences?if + past perfect (had asked), would have + past participle (would have said).
  • What does ‘I should have thought more carefully’ express?Regret or self-criticism. She didn’t think carefully, and now she thinks that was wrong.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: third conditional and should have.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: Third conditional: If + past perfect, would / could / might have + past participle. It describes an imaginary past and its imaginary result. should have / shouldn’t have + past participle = regret or criticism about the past.

CCQs: ‘If I had known, I would have helped.’ Did I know? (No.) Did I help? (No.)

Common mistake: ‘If I would have known...’ No would in the if-clause. Drill the weak form would’ve.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

8 min

Complete the sentences with the correct form of the verb in brackets.

  • If my parents had asked (ask) me, I would have said no.
  • She wouldn’t have posted (not post) the video if she had known it would go viral.
  • If we had used better privacy settings, strangers wouldn’t have seen (not see) the photos.
  • I should have checked (should / check) the location data before I uploaded the picture.
  • They shouldn’t have shared (shouldn’t / share) their son’s school name online.
  • If he hadn’t become (not become) a child star, he might have had a normal childhood.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘Are these sentences about the past or the future?’ (The past.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

16 min

Post or don’t post?

Six parents want to post something about their child. What would you advise?

The tantrum

A video of a 3-year-old screaming in a supermarket. Public account, 2,000 followers.

The prize

A photo of a 10-year-old getting a school prize, in school uniform.

The first bath

A baby’s first bath. Public account.

The haircut

A teenager’s bad haircut, shared in the family group chat only.

The toy review

A sponsored video of a 7-year-old opening new toys. The family is paid.

The beach

A photo of a child at the beach. The face is not visible.

Steps

  1. For each post, decide: fine, fine with changes, or never.
  2. Imagine the parents posted it. What might have happened? What should they have done?
  3. Write three golden rules for parents.
  4. Compare your rules with another pair. Agree on the best three.

Useful language

If they had posted that, ...They should have asked...That’s harmless, as long as...I’d draw the line at...What about the child’s point of view?It depends on who can see it.

Teacher notes. 16 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: Six parents want to post something about their child. Read the cards and decide: fine, fine with changes, or never. Then imagine the consequences.

Pairs. 2 min reading, 8 min discussion, 4 min writing rules and comparing. Push for the third conditional and should have in step 2.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “If I would have known, I would have helped.” > “If I had known, I would have helped.”
  • “Parents shouldn’t to post photos.” > “Parents shouldn’t post photos.”
  • “Children can’t give their agreement for this.” > “Children can’t give consent for this.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

  • Everything you post online becomes part of your digital footprint.
  • Doctors need the patient’s written consent before an operation.
  • Check your privacy settings so that only friends can see your photos.
  • My dad told an embarrassing story about me at my wedding.
  • Some companies exploit young workers by paying them very little.

B. Writing: a for-and-against essay (200-240 words)

‘Parents should not be allowed to post photos of their children online.’ Write a for-and-against essay. Present arguments on both sides and give your own opinion in the conclusion.

Checklist

  • Use the third conditional or should have at least three times.
  • Use five words from the lesson.
  • Use linkers of contrast: although, on the other hand, whereas.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

In a for-and-against essay, the writer’s opinion belongs in the conclusion only.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
