Cancelled! Public Opinion on Trial

A lesson on cancel culture, mob mentality, and who really deserves a second chance — and the grammar of the passive voice you need to talk about it.

Topic: Cancel Culture & Public Opinion ⏲ ~60 minutes

Somebody says the wrong thing. A screenshot is taken. Within hours, a reputation built over twenty years is undone by twenty thousand strangers. Is that justice — or is it a mob? Today, we argue it out — and we learn how to talk about what was said, who was blamed, and who should be forgiven.

Editorial — Grammar Focus

The Passive Voice

Form

The passive voice is built with the verb be + past participle. The tense of be changes to match the time we're talking about; the past participle itself never changes.

subject + BE + past participle (+ by agent)

Modal passives follow the same logic: modal + be + past participle (present/future), or modal + have been + past participle (past/hypothetical).

modal + BE / HAVE BEEN + past participle

Use

We choose the passive when the action or the receiver of the action matters more than who did it — which is exactly how most cancel-culture stories are told: "He was accused..." matters more than naming every single person who accused him. We also use it when the doer is unknown, obvious, or unimportant, or simply to sound more formal, neutral, and journalistic — the classic tone of a news report.

Active vs. Passive, side by side
Tense / StructureActivePassive
Present Simple The internet cancels public figures overnight. Public figures are cancelled overnight (by the internet).
Past Simple Journalists exposed the scandal last week. The scandal was exposed last week.
Present Perfect Fans have forgiven the singer. The singer has been forgiven by fans.
Modal (present/future) We should hold public figures accountable. Public figures should be held accountable.
Modal Perfect (past) The network could have handled this differently. This could have been handled differently (by the network).

In context

  • The comedian's old jokes were unearthed by an anonymous account just days before his film's release.
  • It is often argued that public apologies are written more for damage control than for genuine remorse.
  • modalSome critics believe that no one should be permanently cancelled for a single mistake.
  • modal perfectMany agree the entire situation could have been avoided if the statement had been fact-checked first.
  • By the time the CEO resigned, the story had already been shared by millions of accounts worldwide.

Letters to the Editor — Speaking

The Court of Public Opinion

Work in pairs or small groups. Choose two or three scenario cards below. For each one, decide: should this person be cancelled, forgiven, or something in between? Justify your opinion using passive structures and this week's vocabulary.

The Chef's Old Interview

A celebrity chef was caught making offensive jokes in an interview filmed ten years ago. The clip has just gone viral, and several restaurant chains have cancelled their partnerships with him.

The Leaked Emails

A tech CEO's private emails were leaked, revealing that employees had been mistreated for years. The company's reputation has been seriously damaged, and calls for her resignation are growing.

The Copied Content

A popular influencer has been accused of stealing ideas from smaller, less-known creators. No formal proof has been published yet, but public outcry is already enormous.

The Old Rivalry Posts

An athlete's old social media posts, in which rival fans were openly mocked, have resurfaced right before an important match. He says he was a different person back then.

The Off-Air Comment

A local news anchor was recorded making an insensitive remark off-air. The recording was leaked and shared by a former colleague. The anchor claims it was taken out of context.

The Apology Video

After weeks of backlash, a public figure has finally posted an apology video. Some viewers believe he was genuinely sorry; others think the video was clearly written by his PR team.

Feature Story — Production Task

Your Turn on the Front Page

Prepare a 2–3 minute talk about a real or invented cancel-culture story. Use the points below to structure it.

Guiding Points

  • Introduce the public figure and briefly explain what they are accused of — use passive voice: "It is claimed that...", "He was accused of..."
  • Describe how the story spread: what was shared, what went viral, and how the backlash developed.
  • Explain what has been done so far: apologies made, statements issued, deals cancelled, etc.
  • Give your own opinion: should this person be held accountable, or be given a second chance? Use at least one modal passive.
  • Use a minimum of four vocabulary items from today's lesson naturally in your talk.
