3

Background

10 min

The Case For and Against Zoos

Modern zoos describe themselves as centres for conservation, education and research. Their strongest example is captive breeding. The Arabian oryx was declared extinct in the wild in 1972, but animals bred in captivity were later reintroduced to the deserts of the Arabian Peninsula. In 1987, the last wild California condors were taken into captivity, leaving just 27 birds in existence. Today, hundreds fly free again.

Supporters also argue that zoos inspire millions of visitors, especially children, to care about wildlife they would never otherwise see, and that research in zoos has improved veterinary care for wild populations.

Critics reply that these successes involve a small number of species, and that many zoo animals are not endangered at all. Large, wide-ranging animals are a particular concern. Elephants, polar bears and big cats may travel long distances every day in the wild. In captivity, some develop stereotypic behaviour, such as pacing or swaying, which is widely seen as a sign of stress. One major study found that African elephants in European zoos had a median lifespan less than a third of that of elephants in a protected population in Kenya.

Some campaigners want zoos to be replaced by sanctuaries, which care for animals that cannot survive in the wild but do not breed or display them for profit. Others argue for reform rather than abolition: larger enclosures, a focus on species that genuinely benefit, and an end to keeping the animals that suffer most in captivity.

Answer the questions.

  • What is the strongest argument for zoos, according to the text?Captive breeding, with examples like the Arabian oryx and the California condor.
  • Why are large animals a particular concern?They travel long distances in the wild. In captivity they can show signs of stress and, in some cases, live shorter lives.
  • What is the difference between the ‘abolition’ and ‘reform’ positions?Abolition: replace zoos with sanctuaries. Reform: keep zoos but improve them and stop keeping the animals that suffer most.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the background. Answer the questions. This is the evidence for your debate.

Students highlight facts they could use in a speech.

A model speech

10 min

Opening speech for the motion: ‘This house would close all zoos’

1Ladies and gentlemen, the motion before us today is that all zoos should be closed. I will make three points: first, about welfare; second, about conservation; and finally, about education.

2Let me begin with welfare. An elephant in the wild may walk many kilometres a day. In a zoo, it walks in circles. The evidence of stress is clear, and in some cases, so is the evidence of shorter lives.

3Turning to conservation, our opponents will tell you that zoos save species. We don’t deny that a handful of breeding programmes have succeeded. But they are the exception, not the rule. Most animals in zoos are not endangered, and the money spent on displaying them could protect habitats in the wild, where conservation really matters.

4Finally, education. Is it really education to watch a bear pace back and forth behind glass? What children learn is that it is acceptable to keep wild animals for our entertainment.

5In conclusion, zoos fail animals, they are an inefficient way to protect species, and they teach the wrong lesson. I urge you to support the motion.

Say: What does each phrase do in a debate? Decide with your partner.

Board the five functions. Drill the phrases with confident, formal delivery: slow, clear, with pauses.

Teach the concede-then-rebut pattern: ‘We don’t deny that X. But Y.’ It is the most powerful move in debating.

6

Rebuttal practice

9 min

Write a rebuttal to each argument. Concede first, then counter. (Example answers in Teacher view.)

  • Zoos inspire children to love animals.Granted, many children enjoy zoos. But documentaries and sanctuaries can inspire them without keeping animals in captivity.
  • Without zoos, some species would be extinct.We don’t deny that breeding programmes have saved some species. But they could continue in specialist conservation centres, not public zoos.
  • Modern zoos have large, natural enclosures.Some do. Even so, even the best enclosure is a tiny fraction of a wild animal’s natural range.
  • Zoos fund conservation in the wild.That’s true of some zoos. But the share of income spent on field conservation varies a lot, and it could be raised in other ways.

Teacher notes. 9 min. Pairs.

Say: Practise the key debate move. For each argument, concede, then counter. Then say your rebuttals aloud to your partner.

Accept any well-structured rebuttal. The model answers are examples only.

7

The debate

22 min

Motion: This house would close all zoos.

Speaker 1 (each team)

Opening speech: define the motion and give two or three main arguments. Two minutes.

Speaker 2 (each team)

Rebut the other team’s arguments and add one new point. Two minutes.

Speaker 3 (each team)

Summary: compare the two sides and explain why your team won. Ninety seconds.

Judges (if you have more students)

Score each team on argument, rebuttal and language from 1 to 5.

Steps

  1. Teams prepare for eight minutes. Speakers plan their part together.
  2. Debate order: Proposition 1, Opposition 1, Proposition 2, Opposition 2, Opposition summary, Proposition summary.
  3. Judges give their scores and one reason each.
  4. Repeat the class poll from the warm-up. Has anyone changed their mind?

Useful language

I will make three points.Turning to...We don’t deny that..., but...That argument doesn’t stand up to scrutiny.Can we honestly say that...?I urge you to oppose the motion.

Teacher notes. 22 min. Two teams.

Say: It’s debate time. Proposition supports closing zoos. Opposition is against. You have eight minutes to prepare.

8 min preparation, 12 min debate, 2 min judging and the final poll. Keep strict time. Use a phone timer.

Note good language and errors for each speaker. Give each speaker one piece of feedback.

One-to-one: the student gives the Proposition speeches; you give the Opposition speeches. Then swap sides for the summary.

Reflection and feedback

8 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘I want to say three points.’ > ‘I will make three points.’
  • ‘We can’t deny that, but...’ Fine. Make sure the counter-argument follows.
  • ‘animals in captive’ > ‘animals in captivity’
