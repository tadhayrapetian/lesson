    Alone Among Millions

    A story about a big city and a small pottery class

    Maya moved to the city for a job she had wanted for years. Her flat was small but bright, and from her window she could see hundreds of other windows. At night, every one of them glowed. Behind each one, someone was cooking, arguing or laughing. Maya had never lived so close to so many people. She had also never felt so alone.

    Her experience is more common than you might think. Psychologists often describe loneliness as the gap between the relationships we have and the relationships we want. It has little to do with the number of people around us. You can sit in a packed metro carriage and feel completely invisible.

    Big cities make that gap wider. People are busy and always on the move. In such a transient population, neighbours change every year, and few people learn each other's names. The anonymity that feels like freedom at first can slowly turn into isolation. Many conversations stay at the level of small talk, polite but superficial. We crave a sense of belonging, yet we rarely stay in one place long enough to build it. Old friends drift apart, and new ones take time. The busier the city, the easier it is to hide.

    Technology adds another layer. We follow hundreds of people online and still eat dinner alone. Scrolling through other people's best moments can make our own lives look emptier.

    The good news is that small, repeated contact makes a real difference. Seeing the same barista every morning, joining a weekly running club or volunteering at a local library gives people a chance to move from strangers to familiar faces, and from familiar faces to friends.

    After six months, Maya signed up for a Tuesday evening pottery class. At first she found it hard to open up and barely spoke. Then one evening a classmate asked for help with a broken bowl. They laughed at the mess, went for tea and kept meeting. Today the class is a close-knit group with a shared chat, a birthday list and Friday dinners.

    The city did not get any smaller. Maya found her own place in it. Loneliness is common, and it can change. The first step is usually to reach out, and very often someone nearby wants the same thing.

      Grammar: the more, the more

      8 min

      From the text

      The busier the city, the easier it is to hide.

      We use the + comparative, the + comparative to show that two things change together.

      The more you practise, the better you speak.
      The older I get, the more I value my friends.

      Rewrite the sentences

      Start each sentence with The.
