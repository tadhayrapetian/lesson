The Ethics of True Crime

True crime podcasts and series have millions of fans. Who pays the price for our curiosity?

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • follow a fast, complex debate and infer speakers’ attitudes
  • concede and counter-argue with sophisticated language
  • agree a code of conduct for an ethical issue

Teacher’s overview

Lesson type: Listening. Length: 90 minutes plus homework.

Main aim: Listening: gist, inference and language in a radio debate.

Subsidiary aims: Speaking: an ethics panel. Vocabulary for media, crime and justice.

Language aim: Conceding and countering: I don’t deny that..., That’s a fair criticism, Which is commendable. But..., any more than.

Background: The 2014 podcast Serial brought major attention to a murder conviction, and the case was later reopened. 1

Warm-up

7 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. Do you watch or listen to true crime? Why do you think it’s so popular?
  2. Is it different from reading crime news in a newspaper?
  3. Would you want your family’s story told in a podcast?

Teacher notes. 7 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Today’s topic is true crime. Discuss the questions with your partner.

Note: the topic involves real crimes. Keep the discussion on media ethics. Let students opt out of personal examples.

3

Predict

5 min

You will hear a radio debate between a true crime podcaster and a journalist who works with victims’ families.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Pairs.

Say: Predict the arguments on each side. Write two or three for each.

Keep predictions visible for the first listening.

4

First listening

8 min

The Debate, radio programme
Your teacher will play or read the recording. Listen carefully. You don’t need to understand every word.

Audio script: The Debate, radio programme. Read it aloud at natural speed, or record it in advance with a colleague.

Maya: Good evening and welcome to The Debate. True crime is one of the most popular genres in the world, from podcasts to streaming series. Tonight’s question: is it ethical? I’m joined by Tom Reyes, who presents a true crime podcast, and Ruth Adams, a journalist who works with victims’ families. Tom, why do you think people love these stories?

Tom: I think it’s partly human nature. We’re fascinated by the darkest corners of human behaviour, and we want to understand how ordinary people end up doing terrible things. But I’d also argue that true crime does real good. When it’s done well, it exposes failures in the justice system. The podcast Serial, back in 2014, brought huge attention to a murder conviction, and years later the case was reopened. Without public attention, that might never have happened.

Maya: Ruth, you’re not convinced.

Ruth: Well, I don’t deny that there are examples like that. But they’re the exception rather than the rule. Most true crime isn’t investigative journalism; it’s entertainment. And for the families of victims, it can be devastating. Imagine losing your daughter and then, twenty years later, discovering that her death is the subject of a series that millions of strangers are binge-watching over popcorn. Nobody asked you. Nobody even told you.

Tom: That’s a fair criticism, and I’ll admit some productions have behaved appallingly. In 2022, a hugely popular drama about a serial killer was criticised by victims’ relatives who said they hadn’t been consulted. On my show, we contact families before we cover a case, and if they ask us not to, we don’t.

Ruth: Which is commendable. But you’re one show among thousands. And there’s another problem: amateur detectives. After the Boston Marathon bombing in 2013, people on online forums wrongly identified an innocent man as a suspect. His family was harassed at the worst moment of their lives. Listeners think they’re helping, but they can do real damage.

Maya: Tom, do podcasts encourage that?

Tom: Responsible ones don’t. We tell our audience very clearly: don’t contact people, and don’t speculate about names. But I can’t control what every listener does, any more than a newspaper can.

Maya: So what would ethical true crime look like? Ruth?

Ruth: Centre the victims, not the killers. Get consent from families wherever possible. Don’t turn real violence into a cliffhanger. And if you profit from someone’s tragedy, consider giving something back, for example to support services.

Tom: I’d agree with nearly all of that. I’d add one thing: don’t treat your audience as if they were bloodthirsty. Most listeners care deeply about justice.

Maya: We’ll have to leave it there. Thank you both.

Listen. Answer the questions and check your predictions.

  • What is the main focus of the debate?
  • By the end, the speakers...

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Listen to the debate. Answer the two questions. Check your predictions.

Read the script once at a natural, fairly fast pace. C1 students need authentic speed.

Pairs compare, then check predictions.

5

Second listening

17 min

Listen again. Choose the best answer.

  • Why does Tom mention Serial?
  • What does Ruth mean by ‘the exception rather than the rule’?
  • What is Ruth’s main concern about victims’ families?
  • How does Tom respond to the criticism about families?
  • What happened after the Boston Marathon bombing?
  • What does Tom add to Ruth’s principles?

Teacher notes. 17 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the questions first. Several are about what the speakers mean. Then listen again.

Read the script a second time. For items 2 and 4, replay the exact lines.

Ask: ‘How does Tom’s tone change when he says That’s a fair criticism?’

6

Conceding and countering

13 min

Complete what the speakers said. Use one word in each gap.

  • Well, I don’t deny that there are examples like that. But...
  • That’s a fair criticism, and I’ll admit some productions have behaved appallingly.
  • Which is commendable. But you’re one show among thousands.
  • I’d agree with nearly all of that.
  • I can’t control what every listener does, any more than a newspaper can.

Conceding

  • I don’t deny that...
  • That’s a fair criticism.
  • I’ll admit that...
  • Granted, ...

Countering

  • But they’re the exception rather than the rule.
  • Which is commendable. But...
  • Even so, ...
  • ... any more than...

Agreeing with reservations

  • I’d agree with nearly all of that.
  • I’d add one thing: ...
  • Up to a point.

Teacher notes. 13 min. Pairs.

Say: These phrases are how skilled speakers disagree politely. Complete the lines, then listen again to check.

Read only the target lines. Drill the intonation: a fall on the concession, a pause, then a rise on But.

Highlight the elegant counter ‘Which is commendable. But...’: praise first, then the real point.

7

Speaking: the ethics panel

22 min

You are an ethics panel for a streaming platform. Decide whether these projects should go ahead, then write a code of conduct.

Project 1

A podcast about a 30-year-old unsolved case. The victim’s family supports it and hopes for new information.

Project 2

A drama series about a famous killer, told from his point of view.

Project 3

A podcast that names a suspect who was never charged.

Project 4

A documentary about a possible wrongful conviction. The victim’s family opposes it.

Steps

  1. Discuss each project. Use conceding and countering language.
  2. Decide: approve, approve with conditions, or reject.
  3. Write a five-point code of conduct for true crime on your platform.
  4. Present your decisions and code. Other groups challenge one decision.

Useful language

I don’t deny that..., but...That’s a fair point. Even so, ...Granted, ..., but...It would be commendable if...That’s the exception rather than the rule.I’d agree up to a point.

Teacher notes. 22 min. Groups of three or four.

Say: You’re the ethics panel for a streaming platform. Discuss the four projects and decide. Then write a code of conduct.

Groups. 4 min reading, 12 min discussion, 6 min presenting and challenging.

Project 4 is the hardest: justice for the accused versus the wishes of the victim’s family. Let students feel the dilemma.

One-to-one: the student chairs. You argue the opposite of every decision.

8

Reflection and feedback

6 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘I don’t deny it, but...’ with no clear point. Encourage a full counter-argument.
  • ‘The family wasn’t consulted about it.’ Fine. ‘consulted with them’ needs care: ‘consulted them’.
  • ‘It’s a big exploitation.’ > ‘It’s exploitative.’

Homework

Set at the end

A. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

speculatedevastatingbinge-watchedcliffhangercommendable

  • We binge-watched the whole series in one weekend.

  • The episode ended on a cliffhanger, so I had to watch the next one.

Write a discursive essay (280-320 words).

‘True crime entertainment does more harm than good.’ Discuss, using examples, and reach a clear conclusion.

Checklist

  • Use at least three conceding and countering phrases.
  • Use six words from the lesson.
  • Include a fair concession before your strongest argument.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, complete Part A and write the essay.

Mark for argument quality, concession and refutation, and range of language.
