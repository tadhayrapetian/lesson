Phones Away, Heads Up

Countries around the world are banning phones in schools. Is it working?

Warm-up

6 min

Talk with a partner.

  1. Were phones allowed in your school? What were the rules?
  2. Do phones help or hurt learning? Why?
  3. How would you feel about giving your phone away for six hours every day?

Teacher notes. 6 min. Whole class, then pairs.

Say: Let’s talk about phones and school. Discuss these questions with your partner.

Give students 1 minute to think, then pairs discuss. Take two or three ideas from the class. Do not correct language here. This stage is about interest and ideas.

Say: Great ideas. Today’s text has some useful words for this topic. Let’s look at them first.

3

First read

6 min

Read the text quickly. Choose the best summary.

The End of Phones in Class?

From the Netherlands to Brazil, schools are locking phones away. Teachers report calmer classrooms, but not everyone is convinced.

At a secondary school in Amsterdam, the day starts with a small ritual. Students walk in, switch off their phones and lock them in a magnetic pouch that stays closed until the last bell. ‘At first, everyone hated it,’ says Noor, 15. ‘But now break time is loud again. People actually talk.’

The Netherlands is one of many countries that have decided to prohibit phones in classrooms. Similar rules have been introduced in France, Italy, Brazil, South Korea and parts of the US and Australia, often within just a few years. Although the rules vary, the goal is the same: to help students concentrate and to protect their mental health.

Supporters point to clear benefits. Teachers say students listen better and that there is less cyberbullying during the school day. Some studies suggest that test results improve, especially for weaker students, while others find a smaller effect. Many parents welcome the bans, even though their children complain. They worry that constant notifications are damaging their children’s attention span and adding to their screen time.

Critics, on the other hand, say the evidence is still mixed. Despite the rules, many students simply use their phones in the toilets or hide a second phone. Rules are hard to enforce, and teachers don’t want to spend lessons acting as police or arguing with students whose phones they have to confiscate. Some parents also want to be able to contact their children in an emergency.

There is also a bigger question. Whereas schools once taught students how to use technology, some now seem to be avoiding it. ‘Banning phones is easy,’ says one education researcher. ‘Teaching digital literacy, how to use a phone wisely, is much harder. Students will have phones for the rest of their lives. School should prepare them for that.’

Teacher notes. 6 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the text quickly. Don’t stop at new words. Choose the best summary. You have 3 minutes.

ICQs: “Do you need to understand every word?” (No.) “How many answers do you choose?” (One.)

Set a strict time limit. Pairs compare. In feedback ask: “Which part of the text helped you?”

Answer: C. Benefits in paragraph 3, criticism in paragraph 4, a bigger question in paragraph 5.

Say: Now read again, more carefully this time.

4

Close read

10 min

Read again. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)?

  • Noor liked the new rule from the start.
  • Test results in the Netherlands have improved since the ban.
  • Some studies suggest weaker students benefit more.
  • Students at Noor’s school can use their phones at lunch.
  • Some parents want to be able to contact their children.
  • The researcher thinks schools should teach students to use phones well.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read again carefully. Are the sentences true (T), false (F) or not given (NG)? Underline the part of the text that gives you the answer.

ICQ: “What does not given mean?” (The text doesn’t say.)

Pairs compare and show each other the evidence. In feedback, always ask “Where does it say that?”

Say: Well done. Now let’s look closely at some language from the text.

5

Language focus

12 min

Look at these sentences from the text.

  • Although the rules vary, the goal is the same.
  • Many parents welcome the bans, even though their children complain.
  • Despite the rules, many students simply use their phones in the toilets.
  • Some studies suggest that test results improve, especially for weaker students, while others find a smaller effect.
  • Whereas schools once taught students how to use technology, some now seem to be avoiding it.

Answer the questions with a partner.

  • Which linkers are followed by a full clause (subject + verb)?although, even though, while, whereas
  • What follows despite?A noun or an -ing form: despite the rules, despite having rules.
  • Which linkers compare two different facts, rather than show surprise?while and whereas

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs, then whole class.

Focus: linkers of contrast.

Say: Look at the sentences from the text. Work with a partner and answer the questions. Don’t worry about rules yet. Just look for patterns.

Monitor. Then elicit answers and build the rule on the board with the students. Students tell you the rule. You write it.

Rule for the board: although / even though + clause = contrast or surprise (even though is stronger). despite / in spite of + noun or -ing. while / whereas + clause = compare two different facts. however / on the other hand start a new sentence or clause and are followed by a comma.

CCQs: ‘Despite the rain, we played.’ Was it raining? (Yes.) Did we play? (Yes.) Is that a little surprising? (Yes.)

Common mistakes: ‘despite of the rules’ and ‘although the rules’ + noun. Also ‘Although..., but...’. English uses only one linker here.

Say: Now let’s practise it.

6

Practice

8 min

Join the sentences with the word given. Make any changes you need.

  • The school banned phones. Students still used them.despiteDespite the ban, students still used their phones.
  • I hated the rule at first. Now I like it.althoughAlthough I hated the rule at first, I like it now.
  • My school allows phones at lunch. My cousin’s school doesn’t.whereasMy school allows phones at lunch, whereas my cousin’s school doesn’t.
  • She was tired. She finished her homework.even thoughShe finished her homework even though she was tired.
  • The rule was difficult to enforce. It worked.in spite ofIn spite of being difficult to enforce, the rule worked.
  • Teachers support the ban. Some parents are against it.whileWhile teachers support the ban, some parents are against it.

Teacher notes. 8 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Work alone first. Complete the sentences. Then check with your partner.

ICQs: ‘Alone or with a partner first?’ (Alone.) ‘After despite, can you use a full clause like they were tired?’ (No. A noun or -ing.)

Fast finishers write one more sentence of their own with the target language.

In feedback, write the answers on the board. Focus on form errors. Ask “Why this form?” for one or two items.

Say: Perfect. Now you’re ready to use all of this in a real conversation.

7

Speaking

16 min

The school board

You are on the board of a school with students aged 7 to 17. Choose the best phone policy for younger and older students.

Total ban

Phones stay at home or in lockers all day.

Pouch system

Phones are locked in pouches during school hours.

Lessons only

Phones are allowed at break time, but not in lessons.

Teacher’s choice

Each teacher decides the rules for their own class.

No ban, more education

Phones are allowed. Every class has a weekly digital literacy lesson.

Steps

  1. Read the five policies.
  2. For each one, find one advantage and one disadvantage. Use contrast linkers.
  3. Choose one policy for students aged 7 to 11 and one for students aged 12 to 17.
  4. Present your choices and justify any difference.

Useful language

Although it’s strict, ...Despite the advantages, ...Whereas younger children..., teenagers...The main drawback is...I’d go for... because...That might work in theory, but...

Teacher notes. 16 min. Pairs or small groups.

Say: You’re on the school board. Read the five phone policies. For each one, find an advantage and a disadvantage. Then choose one policy for younger students and one for older students.

Pairs or small groups. 3 min reading, 8 min discussion, 4 min presentations. Ask groups to justify any difference between the two age groups.

Give clear preparation time before students speak. Monitor closely. Write down good language and errors in two columns. Do not interrupt. Fluency comes first here.

One-to-one: you take the other role or position. Keep your turns short and push the student to justify and expand (“Why?”, “Can you give an example?”).

Say: Time’s up. Let’s look at some of the language you used.

8

Reflection and feedback

5 min

Think about today's lesson.

Teacher notes. 5 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board: some correct, some with errors. Do not say who said them.

Say: Here are some sentences I heard. Some are perfect. Some have a mistake. Work with your partner and find the mistakes.

Students correct in pairs. Elicit the corrections. Praise good language and highlight two or three great phrases students used.

Errors students often make with this lesson:

  • “Despite of the ban, they used phones.” > “Despite the ban, they used phones.”
  • “Although the rule is strict, but it works.” > “Although the rule is strict, it works.”
  • “The teacher took off my phone.” > “The teacher took my phone away.”

Then students complete the reflection boxes. Ask one or two students to read theirs aloud.

9

Homework

Set at the end

A. Vocabulary review. Complete the sentences with words from the lesson. Change the form if you need to.

confiscateattention spanevidenceconcentrateenforce
  • I can’t concentrate when my phone is on the desk.
  • There is no scientific evidence that this diet works.
  • The police find it hard to enforce the new speed limit.
  • The teacher will confiscate your phone if you use it during the test.
  • Watching short videos all day may shorten your attention span.

B. Writing: an opinion essay (200-240 words)

‘Smartphones should be completely banned in all schools.’ To what extent do you agree? Write an essay with an introduction, arguments for and against, and a conclusion.

Checklist

  • Use at least four different contrast linkers.
  • Use five words from the lesson.
  • Make your position clear in the introduction and the conclusion.

Teacher notes. Set at the end, 2 min. Individual.

Say: For homework, do Part A and Part B. In Part B, use at least four words from today and the grammar we studied.

Collect the writing next lesson. Mark it for four things: task completion, vocabulary from the lesson, accuracy with the target language, and organisation.

‘To what extent’ essays need a clear position from the start, unlike for-and-against essays.

Start the next lesson with a two-minute vocabulary quiz using the words from Part A.
