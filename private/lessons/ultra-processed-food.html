What’s Really on Your Plate?

Ultra-processed food is cheap, tasty and everywhere. Learn the language to talk about what we eat.

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • use precise vocabulary to talk about food, health and diet
  • build word families with suffixes (nutrition, nutritious, nutritional)
  • use food collocations and compound adjectives naturally

3

Read

10 min

The Food That Isn’t Quite Food

Look at the ingredients list on a packet of supermarket bread. Flour, water, yeast, salt... and then a list of words you can’t pronounce.

Emulsifiers, preservatives, flavour enhancers: welcome to the world of ultra-processed food, or UPF. The term comes from a system created by Brazilian scientists, who divided food into four groups according to how processed it is. Fresh fruit is unprocessed. Cheese and home-made bread are processed. But ultra-processed foods are made mostly from industrial ingredients and additives you would never find in a home kitchen. Think fizzy drinks, packaged snacks, many breakfast cereals and ready-made meals.

In countries like the UK and the US, more than half of the calories people eat now come from UPF. These foods are cheap, convenient and have a long shelf life. They are also designed to be delicious, which may be why it’s so easy to eat a large portion and still have cravings an hour later.

Many studies link diets high in UPF to obesity, heart disease and other health problems. Some scientists warn, though, that the category is too broad: a packet of crisps and a wholegrain supermarket loaf can both count as UPF, but they are not equally unhealthy.

So what should we do? Most experts agree that you don’t need to panic about every additive. Cooking more meals from scratch, reading labels and eating UPF in moderation is a realistic place to start.

Answer the questions in your own words.

  • Where does the UPF classification come from?Brazilian scientists created a system with four groups based on processing.
  • What makes a food ‘ultra-processed’?It’s made mostly from industrial ingredients and additives that you wouldn’t use at home.
  • Give two reasons why UPF is so popular.It’s cheap, convenient, has a long shelf life and is designed to be delicious.
  • Why do some scientists think the category is too broad?Very different foods, like crisps and wholegrain bread, can both be UPF, but they aren’t equally unhealthy.

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Read the article and answer the questions. Notice the new words.

Students underline every word from stage 2 in the text.

5

Word formation

10 min

Complete each sentence with the correct form of the word in capitals.

  • Lentils are cheap and very nutritious. (NUTRITION)
  • The nutritional information is on the back of the packet. (NUTRITION)
  • Food processing is a huge industry. (PROCESS)
  • Many preservatives are added to keep food fresh. (PRESERVE)
  • There’s no scientific evidence that this additive is dangerous. (SCIENCE)
  • Too much sugar can be harmful to your teeth. (HARM)
  • Fast food is popular because of its convenience. (CONVENIENT)

Teacher notes. 10 min. Individual, then pairs.

Say: Change the word in capitals so it fits the sentence. First decide: do you need a noun, an adjective or a verb?

Word formation is a key task in B2 exams like FCE. Teach the strategy: look at the word before and after the gap.

Board the suffixes: -ous, -al, -ing, -ive, -ific, -ful, -ence.

7

Speaking: you and your food

12 min

Discuss the questions. Use words from the lesson.

  1. How often do you cook from scratch?
  2. What food do you crave when you’re tired or stressed?
  3. Have you ever gone on a diet? Did it work?
  4. Should ultra-processed food have warning labels, like cigarettes?
  5. Is wholesome food too expensive where you live?

Then design a realistic one-week challenge to cut down on UPF for a busy student.

Useful language

I try to cut down on...When I’m stressed, I crave...It’s fine in moderation.The problem is the convenience.A realistic first step would be...I’m not sure that’s affordable.

Teacher notes. 12 min. Pairs.

Say: Discuss the questions with your partner. Then design a one-week challenge for a busy student.

8 min discussion, 4 min challenge design. Pairs share their challenge.

Monitor for collocations and word forms. Note good examples to praise.

Reflection and feedback

3 min

Think about today’s lesson.

Teacher notes. 3 min. Whole class.

Delayed error correction. Write four or five sentences you heard on the board, some correct and some with errors. Students correct them in pairs. Then they complete the reflection boxes.

Common errors in this lesson:

  • ‘make a diet’ > ‘go on a diet’
  • ‘This food is very nutrition.’ > ‘This food is very nutritious.’
